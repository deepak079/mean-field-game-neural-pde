AMS 516 · Problem 21 · the extension

The league of N investors

The same problem, but with a few investors who see each other. This guide explains it from scratch: the exact model, the game theory it needs, the equation of each investor, how it is solved with one network trained in rounds, and how it is checked.

Colours: each one always means the same thing

  • Timewhat changes because the clock moves: \(\partial_t\)
  • The predictable partthe drift of a wealth: \(rx + \pi(\mu - r)\)
  • Luckthe noise, \((\sigma\pi)^2\), and the curvature
  • My decisionhow much I invest: \(\pi\)
  • The rivalstheir wealths \(y_j\), their mean \(\bar y\) and what they invest, \(\pi_j\)

1The map

What we build, on one screen.

In the base problem there are infinitely many investors, and each of them needs only one number from the rest: the final mean \(\cM{\bar m_T}\). In the league there are \(N\) investors, from 2 to 20, and each one sees every rival's wealth at every moment. How much an investor invests depends on their own wealth and on everyone else's: \(N\) variables at once. From \(N = 4\) on, that is too many for a finite-difference grid, but not for a network.

The recipe:

  1. Write the equation of an investor who knows the rivals' rule. It is an HJB like the 1-D one, with one more term per rival.§4
  2. Give it a symmetric network. A network that does not care in which order the rivals are passed to it. The same network serves everyone.§7
  3. Train it where the group lives. On situations drawn around the population, with no box and no edges.§8§9
  4. Play in rounds. The rivals play the rule of the round before and the network learns the best response. This is repeated until the rule only changes by training noise.§6
  5. Check the minimum. Exact tests without training and one trained exact case. The rest (finite differences with N = 2, Monte Carlo of the value, a best response from scratch) is optional.§10
  6. Measure what changes with N. The final mean, the spread and how much the group crowds together, for N = 2, 3, 5, 10 and 20, against the mean-field. The plan is in configs/nplayer.yaml and scripts/analyze_nplayer.py.

For each \(N\) the result is a network that gives my value \(V(t, x, y)\) and my rule \(\pi(t, x, y)\) in any realistic situation, plus the group's statistics and the figures.

2The model, with no ambiguity

The usual statement with finite \(N\), plus the only new assumption: everyone sees everyone's wealth.

The investors

There are \(N\) identical investors, \(i = 1, \dots, N\). Each one has a wealth \(X^i_t\) and puts \(\pi^i_t\) dollars in their stock. Each one's luck is independent of the others', as in equation (1) of the proposal:

\[ dX^i_t = \cB{\big(rX^i_t + \cP{\pi^i_t}(\mu - r)\big)\,dt} + \cR{\sigma\,\cP{\pi^i_t}\,dW^i_t}, \qquad W^1, \dots, W^N \text{ independent}. \]

Initial wealths: each one drawn on its own, \(X^i_0 \sim \mathcal N(1, 0.2^2)\). Base parameters: \(r = 0.02\), \(\mu = 0.08\), \(\sigma = 0.2\), \(\gamma = 1\), \(T = 1\) and \(\lambda = 2\).

What each one wants

To maximise \(\mathbb E\big[U(X^i_T, \bar X_T)\big]\), with the group's final mean, in which each investor also counts:

\[ \bar X_T = \frac1N\sum_{k=1}^N X^k_T, \qquad U(x, \bar x) = -e^{-\gamma x} - \frac{\lambda}{2}\Big(\big(\bar x - x\big)^+\Big)^2. \]

The utility has two parts:

  • \(-e^{-\gamma x}\): the exponential utility of one's own wealth, the usual one. It is always negative, more wealth brings it closer to 0, and \(\gamma\) measures how much risk is disliked.
  • \(-\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\big((\bar x - x)^+\big)^2\): the penalty for ending below the mean. The \(^+\) means “if negative, 0”: if I end above the mean, there is no penalty; if I end 0.2 below, it subtracts \(\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\cdot 0.2^2\). At \(x = \bar x\) the utility has a kink: its curvature jumps, because on one side there is a penalty and on the other there is none.

We already saw what it causes in the mean-field with λ = 2. Just below the mean it pays to be careful, because a bad shock that leaves you further down hurts more than a good one helps: at the end people invest about 0.2, against 1.5 just above. So many people end up crowded just below the mean, and the final density has a peak there that drops suddenly at the mean: a cliff.

What each one can see

Every investor sees everyone's wealth at every moment: the ranking is public. Their rule can depend on the time, on their own wealth and on their rivals' wealths:

\[ \cP{\pi^i_t} = \pi\big(t,\ X^i_t,\ \text{wealths of the rivals of } i\big). \]

This is called a feedback (or Markov) rule: it reacts to the situation at each moment.

What we are looking for

One single rule \(\pi(t, x, y)\) that everyone uses, each from their own seat, and that is the best for each one if the others use it. It is a symmetric Nash equilibrium; section 3 explains what that is.

The penalty, against my rivals

My rule looks at my rivals, so it is convenient to write the penalty with the mean of my \(N - 1\) rivals, \(\bar y\), instead of the group's mean. The group's mean mixes my wealth with theirs:

\[ \bar x = \frac{x + (N-1)\,\bar y}{N} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bar x - x = \frac{x + (N-1)\,\bar y - Nx}{N} = \Big(1 - \frac1N\Big)(\bar y - x). \]

Since \(1 - 1/N\) is positive, it does not change the sign, and squaring gives its square:

\[ \frac{\lambda}{2}\Big(\big(\bar x - x\big)^+\Big)^2 = \frac{\cM{\lambda_N}}{2}\Big(\big(\cM{\bar y} - x\big)^+\Big)^2, \qquad \cM{\lambda_N} = \lambda\Big(1 - \frac1N\Big)^2. \]

So \(U = -e^{-\gamma x} - \tfrac{\lambda_N}{2}\big((\bar y - x)^+\big)^2\): our 1-D utility with \(\bar m\) replaced by \(\bar y\) and \(\lambda\) by \(\lambda_N\). In the code it is the same function, utility(x, ybar, p.with_(lam=lam_N)). Against my rivals the penalty is milder than \(\lambda\), because when I go up, the mean goes up with me.

Check it by hand

\(N = 5\), \(\lambda = 2\), \(x = 0.9\) and my four rivals with mean \(\bar y = 1.1\). The group's mean is \((0.9 + 4 \cdot 1.1)/5 = 1.06\), 0.16 away from me, and the penalty is \(\tfrac{2}{2}\cdot 0.16^2 = 0.0256\). With the rewrite: \(\lambda_5 = 2 \cdot 0.8^2 = 1.28\) and \(\tfrac{1.28}{2}\cdot 0.2^2 = 0.0256\). The same.

3Just enough game theory

Six ideas, applied to our case.

  1. Game. Several players, and what each one gets depends on what everyone does. Here, my final utility depends on my wealth and on the group's mean, which depends on what each one invests.
  2. Rule, or strategy. What to do in each situation. Here, \(\pi(t, x, y)\): how many dollars to invest given the time, my wealth and my rivals' wealths.
  3. Best response. If I know my rivals' rule, the best rule for me. Computing it is a control problem like the 1-D one, an HJB, with \(N\) variables instead of one.
  4. Nash equilibrium. A set of rules in which nobody gains by changing theirs while the others keep theirs. In other words, each rule is the best response to the others.
  5. Symmetric. All investors are identical, so we look for an equilibrium in which everyone uses the same rule, each from their own seat: "me" in the first slot and my rivals in the others.
  6. Rounds (in the literature, fictitious play). Start from a rule, compute the best response, give it to everyone and repeat. If the rule stops changing, it is the best response to itself, and that is an equilibrium.

An exact example, with the original utility of the statement

With \(U = -\exp\!\big(-\gamma(x - \eta\bar x)\big)\), \(\eta = 0.5\) and \(N = 5\), my wealth is one fifth of the mean, so comparing myself with it is partly comparing myself with myself. What is left is like having a lower risk aversion: \(\gamma(1 - \eta/N) = 0.9\).

Round 1. The rivals play Merton: 1.47 at \(t = 0\). My best response is Merton with aversion 0.9: \(1.47/0.9 = 1.63\).

Round 2. The rivals play 1.63. My best response is still 1.63, because it does not depend on which fixed rule they use. The rule does not change: equilibrium in two rounds.

It matches the one of Lacker and Zariphopoulou paper and what Appendix A of our proposal already said. We will use it as an exact test (§10).

What if the rounds do not converge?

There is no general guarantee: there are classic games in which the rounds go round in circles forever. Here we expect them to converge because the interaction is weak. In the mean-field, a wrong guess of the final mean moved the result about 15 times less (\(\Phi' \approx -0.065\)), and the loop closed in 3 evaluations. Section 6 sets what to do if they do not converge.

The mean-field is the limit

With infinitely many rivals, none of them weighs on its own and their mean stops being random (law of large numbers). My rule no longer needs to look at anyone in particular, only at the number \(\bar m_T\): that is our 1-D problem. Section 4 shows it in the equation.

A note on rules that look at everyone

Lacker and Zariphopoulou only look for constant rules, decided at \(t = 0\). For constant rules it makes no difference whether the situation at each moment is observed or not. We let the rule react to the ranking, because with the penalty on the mean there is no reason for it to be constant.

4The equation of each investor

what the V network has to satisfy

A single HJB: the 1-D one with one more term per rival. It is derived in four steps; the last one writes it with \(v = \log(-V)\), which is what the network learns.

One HJB, not N

In a game of \(N\) there are \(N\) control problems, one per investor. Since all are identical and we want everyone to use the same rule, the \(N\) problems are the same equation seen from different seats. It is enough to solve mine; each rival's is the same function with the roles swapped.

Compared with the 1-D HJB, four things change: there are \(N\) wealths instead of one, each rival adds two terms, the final condition uses \(\bar y\) and \(\lambda_N\), and the rivals' positions come from the solution itself. This last point is what makes it a game. Below, subscripts are derivatives: \(V_x = \partial V/\partial x\), \(V_{y_jy_j} = \partial^2 V/\partial y_j^2\).

Step 1 · What \(V\) is, and its value at the end

\(V(t, x, y)\) is the best expected utility I can get from the situation \((t, x, y)\), if my rivals follow their rule. At \(T\) there is nothing left to decide, so the value is the utility, written with \(\bar y\) and \(\lambda_N\) as in section 2:

\[ V(T, x, y) = -e^{-\gamma x} - \frac{\cM{\lambda_N}}{2}\Big(\big(\cM{\bar y} - x\big)^+\Big)^2. \]

Step 2 · How much \(V\) changes in an instant

In an instant \(dt\), my wealth and each rival's move. As in 1-D (Itô's formula), \(V\) changes on average because of the clock, because of each slope times the drift of that wealth, and because of half the curvature times the variance of that wealth:

\[ \frac{\mathbb E[dV]}{dt} = \cT{V_t} + \cB{\big(rx + \cP{\pi}(\mu - r)\big)V_x} + \cR{\tfrac12\sigma^2\cP{\pi}^2\,V_{xx}} + \sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\Big[\cB{\big(r\cM{y_j} + \cM{\pi_j}(\mu - r)\big)V_{y_j}} + \cR{\tfrac12\sigma^2\cM{\pi_j}^2\,V_{y_jy_j}}\Big]. \]

Each rival moves with the same equation as me, with their own position \(\pi_j\). There are no cross terms, such as \(V_{xy_j}\), because each one's luck is independent.

The derivation, step by step

It is a Taylor expansion, as in calculus, with one difference: the luck of an instant \(dt\) has size \(\sqrt{dt}\), much larger than \(dt\), so the squared terms cannot be thrown away. Four steps.

1 · Taylor to second order. In an instant, the clock moves by \(dt\), my wealth by \(dX\) and each rival's by \(dY_j\). As with one variable (the change is the derivative times the step, plus half the second derivative times the step squared), but with all the variables and their products:

\[ dV = V_t\,dt + V_x\,dX + \sum_j V_{y_j}\,dY_j + \tfrac12 V_{xx}\,(dX)^2 + \tfrac12\sum_j V_{y_jy_j}\,(dY_j)^2 + \sum_j V_{xy_j}\,dX\,dY_j + \tfrac12\sum_{j \ne k} V_{y_jy_k}\,dY_j\,dY_k + \dots \]

The dots are even smaller terms, such as \(V_{tt}\,dt^2\) or \(V_{tx}\,dt\,dX\).

2 · The size of each piece. My wealth moves by \(dX = \cB{(rx + \cP{\pi}(\mu - r))\,dt} + \cR{\sigma\cP{\pi}\,dW}\), where the luck \(dW\) is a normal with mean 0 and variance \(dt\): its typical size is \(\sqrt{dt}\). With \(dt = 0.01\), \(dW\) is of order ±0.1, and the products that appear are:

ProductSize with dt = 0.01Kept as
\(dt \cdot dt\)0.00010
\(dt \cdot dW\)±0.0010
\((dW)^2\)0.01\(dt\)
\(dW \cdot dW^j\) (independent noises)±0.010

The first two are much smaller than \(dt\) and, added up along the whole path, leave nothing. \((dW)^2\) is of the size of \(dt\) and is never negative: added up over a stretch of time, it gives that stretch of time. The product of two independent noises is as often positive as negative, and cancels out.

3 · The squares that remain. Squaring \(dX\) gives three pieces, \((\text{drift})^2\,dt^2\), \(2\,(\text{drift})\,\sigma\pi\,dt\,dW\) and \(\sigma^2\pi^2\,(dW)^2\), and only the last one remains. The same for each rival, with their own luck \(dW^j\):

\[ (dX)^2 = \cR{\sigma^2\cP{\pi}^2\,dt}, \qquad (dY_j)^2 = \cR{\sigma^2\cM{\pi_j}^2\,dt}, \qquad dX\,dY_j = 0, \qquad dY_j\,dY_k = 0 \ \ (j \ne k). \]

The cross products are 0 because each wealth has its own luck: that is why \(V_{xy_j}\) does not appear in the HJB.

4 · Substitute and take the mean. Putting \(dX\), \(dY_j\) and the squares into step 1:

\[ dV = \Big[\cT{V_t} + \cB{(rx + \cP{\pi}(\mu - r))V_x} + \cR{\tfrac12\sigma^2\cP{\pi}^2V_{xx}} + \sum_j\Big(\cB{(r\cM{y_j} + \cM{\pi_j}(\mu - r))V_{y_j}} + \cR{\tfrac12\sigma^2\cM{\pi_j}^2V_{y_jy_j}}\Big)\Big]dt + \cR{\sigma\cP{\pi}V_x\,dW} + \sum_j \cR{\sigma\cM{\pi_j}V_{y_j}\,dW^j}. \]

The luck terms have mean 0, so the last two terms disappear when taking the mean. What is left, divided by \(dt\), is the formula above.

Step 3 · The HJB

If I play the best, \(V\) does not change on average along the way: what I expect to get is already counted in \(V\). If I play anything else, it goes down. So the expression above is 0 with my best \(\pi\) and negative with any other one. I only choose my \(\pi\); the \(\pi_j\) are the rivals' rule:

\[ \cT{V_t} + \max_{\pi}\Big\{ \cB{\big(rx + \cP{\pi}(\mu - r)\big)V_x} + \cR{\tfrac12\sigma^2\cP{\pi}^2\,V_{xx}} \Big\} + \sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\Big[ \cB{\big(r\cM{y_j} + \cM{\pi_j}(\mu - r)\big)V_{y_j}} + \cR{\tfrac12\sigma^2\cM{\pi_j}^2\,V_{y_jy_j}} \Big] = 0. \]

Step 4 · The form the network learns

\(V\) is negative and changes like an exponential of wealth; its logarithm is almost a straight line, easier for a network. As in 1-D, the network learns \(v = \log(-V)\), that is, \(V = -e^{v}\). By the chain rule:

\[ V_t = -e^{v}v_t, \qquad V_x = -e^{v}v_x, \qquad V_{xx} = -e^{v}\big(v_{xx} + v_x^2\big), \]

and the same in each \(y_j\). The \(v_x^2\) appears when \(-e^{v}v_x\) is differentiated again: \(e^{v}\) releases another \(v_x\). After substituting, the whole HJB is multiplied by \(-e^{v}\), which is negative; dividing by it, the maximum over \(\pi\) becomes a minimum. My part with \(\pi\) is a parabola:

\[ \min_{\pi}\Big\{\cP{\pi}(\mu - r)\,v_x + \tfrac12\sigma^2\cP{\pi}^2\big(v_{xx} + v_x^2\big)\Big\}. \]

Its minimum is where the derivative in \(\pi\) vanishes, \((\mu - r)v_x + \sigma^2\pi\,(v_{xx} + v_x^2) = 0\). Substituting gives its value (with \(\kappa = (\mu - r)/\sigma\)):

\[ \cP{\pi^*(t, x, y)} = -\frac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\,\frac{v_x}{v_{xx} + v_x^2}, \qquad \text{minimum} = -\frac{\kappa^2}{2}\,\frac{v_x^2}{v_{xx} + v_x^2}. \]

This needs \(v_{xx} + v_x^2 \gt 0\), which is the same as \(V\) being concave: each extra dollar helps a little less. Putting everything together:

\[ \cT{v_t} + \cB{r x\, v_x} - \frac{\kappa^2}{2}\,\frac{v_x^2}{v_{xx} + v_x^2} + \sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\Big[ \cB{\big(r\cM{y_j} + \cM{\pi_j}(\mu - r)\big)\,v_{y_j}} + \cR{\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\cM{\pi_j}^2\big(v_{y_jy_j} + v_{y_j}^2\big)} \Big] = 0, \] \[ v(T, x, y) = \log\!\Big(e^{-\gamma x} + \frac{\lambda_N}{2}\big((\bar y - x)^+\big)^2\Big). \]

The first three terms are those of the 1-D V network (residual_formula in pinn/hjb.py). The sum over the rivals is the new part: the same two terms for each rival, passed through the same chain rule. The final condition is \(\log(-U)\).

Check it by hand: with λ = 0 you get Merton

Without the penalty, \(v = -\gamma e^{r(T-t)}x - \tfrac12\kappa^2(T - t)\), with no rivals. Then \(v_t = r\gamma e^{r(T-t)}x + \tfrac12\kappa^2\), \(r x v_x = -r\gamma e^{r(T-t)}x\), \(v_{xx} = 0\) and \(v_{y_j} = 0\). The third term is \(-\kappa^2/2\), and everything adds up to 0. The rule: \(\pi^* = -1.5\,v_x/v_x^2 = 1.5\,e^{-r(T-t)}\), which gives 1.47 at \(t = 0\).

The seat of each rival

Rival \(j\) uses the same rule, from their own seat: their wealth goes in the own slot and the others, mine included, in the rivals' slots:

\[ \cM{\pi_j} = \pi\big(t,\ y_j,\ y^{(j)}\big), \qquad y^{(j)} = (x,\ y_1, \dots, y_{j-1},\ y_{j+1}, \dots, y_{N-1}). \]

For example, with \(N = 4\), \(x = 1\) and \(y = (2, 3, 4)\), the rival who has 3 sees themselves with 3 and sees their rivals with \(\{1, 2, 4\}\), mean 7/3. It is the easiest step to program wrongly (forgetting my wealth, leaving theirs in), and the exact solutions would not detect it, because their rules do not depend on wealth. That is why a single piece of code builds every seat, in training and in the Monte Carlo, and a test checks this example (§10).

Where the equilibrium is in this equation

Inside the sum, the rivals use a rule \(\pi\). Out of the equation comes my best response \(\pi^*\). There is an equilibrium when the two coincide: the rule used inside is the one that comes out. The rounds of section 6 look for that match.

Three effects the mean-field does not have

  1. My wealth moves the mean. Against my rivals, the penalty weighs \(\lambda_N = \lambda(1 - 1/N)^2\): 25% of λ with N = 2, 64% with 5 and 81% with 10.
  2. The bar is uncertain. Before \(T\) I do not know where my rivals' mean will end. Its standard deviation at the end is about \(0.28/\sqrt{N-1}\), with 0.28 the spread of final wealth in the mean-field: ±0.09 with 10 investors. It should smooth the cliff more than a milder penalty (effect 1) already does; the analysis separates the two by comparing with the mean-field at \(\lambda_N\) (scripts/analyze_nplayer.py).
  3. The rule looks at the ranking. How much I invest depends on whether I am ahead of or behind my rivals, not only on my wealth.

With infinitely many rivals we get our 1-D problem

If \(N \to \infty\), \(\lambda_N \to \lambda\) and each rival weighs \(1/(N-1)\). At the equilibrium the rivals are spread like the population, so, by the law of large numbers, their mean stops being random and follows a fixed path \(\bar m_t\). Then my value depends on the rivals only through their mean, \(v(t, x, y) \approx \tilde v(t, x, \bar y)\), and the sum over the rivals becomes:

\[ \sum_j \big(r y_j + \pi_j(\mu - r)\big)\frac{\tilde v_{\bar y}}{N-1} \;\to\; \frac{d\bar m_t}{dt}\,\tilde v_{\bar y}, \qquad \sum_j \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\pi_j^2\,\frac{\tilde v_{\bar y\bar y} + \tilde v_{\bar y}^2}{(N-1)^2} \;\to\; 0. \]

Along \(\bar y = \bar m_t\), the function \(v_1(t, x) = \tilde v(t, x, \bar m_t)\) satisfies \(\partial_t v_1 = \tilde v_t + \frac{d\bar m_t}{dt}\,\tilde v_{\bar y}\). The equation reduces to the 1-D HJB with \(V(T, x) = U(x, \bar m_T)\): the base problem. That is why the mean-field is the reference for large N.

Why there is no Fokker–Planck here

In the mean-field, the population's density said where the crowd is. In the league, the rivals are already inside the equation: their wealths are variables, and their terms say how they will move. The HJB alone defines the equilibrium. To see how the group ends up (mean, spread, how much it crowds together), games are simulated with Monte Carlo.

5Why finite differences are not enough

A grid needs points in every variable at once; a network does not.

Our FD solver puts 800 points on the wealth axis. With \(N\) wealths, the grid of a single instant has \(800^N\) points, and each point takes 8 bytes:

Npoints per instantmemory per instantFD?
1 (Merton)8006 KByes
26.4·10⁵5 MByes: it is our reference
35.1·10⁸4 GBbarely
44.1·10¹¹3 TBno
101.1·10²⁹—no
201.2·10⁵⁸—no

Even with only 100 points per axis, N = 5 needs 10¹⁰ points: 80 GB per instant. This is the curse of dimensionality: the cost is multiplied with each new variable.

The network has no grid. It learns on drawn points (§8), and only where the group lives, not in the whole cube. Each point costs more the larger \(N\) is: \(N - 1\) rival curvatures must be computed, and each one goes through the \(N - 1\) rivals, so the cost per point grows between \(N\) and \(N^2\). Even so, \(N = 20\) fits in one night.

6The rounds

src/mfg/pinn/nplayer.py · solve_nplayer_equilibrium

How the equilibrium is searched for: the same scheme as the mean-field loop, but what passes from one turn to the next is a rule, not a number.

rivals ← Merton                                   # round 1: π_j = 1.5·e^(−r(T−t)), merton_pi
net ← new symmetric network (with its seed)
π_before ← Merton
for k = 1, 2, …, 6:
    settings ← full if k = 1, short otherwise     # 10,000 Adam + 500 L-BFGS  |  2,000 Adam + 200 L-BFGS
    train(net, rivals, settings)                  # loss of §9; the rivals' π_j without gradient
    π_k ← the network's rule on the check states
    Δ_k ← mean of |π_k − π_before|
    save(net, Δ_k)                                # a relaunch continues from here
    if k ≥ 3 and Δ_k ≥ Δ_(k−1)/2: stop            # no longer halves: only noise is left
    rivals ← frozen copy of the network
    π_before ← π_k
accept if the last Δ < 0.02                       # otherwise, or after 6 rounds: look at it before using it

Decisions, and why

Start with the rivals at Merton
It is exact and smooth, does not depend on anything trained, and is the starting point of the mean-field loop (the λ = 0 mean).
Round 1 in full, the others short
The network starts at random, so round 1 uses the default 1-D settings. The others continue from the previous network with the 1-D warm-start settings (warm_stage). Han and Hu found that each round does not need to be solved precisely if the network keeps training from one round to the next paper.
Rivals = frozen copy of the previous round
During a round, my best-response problem stays fixed and well defined. The last round is used, as in Han and Hu, and not an average of several: the loop contracts strongly (in the mean-field, each turn divided the error by about 15), so the last round is already the best estimate, and mixing it with earlier ones would only slow things down.
Each rival's π, from the frozen copy
\(\pi_j = \min\!\Big(10,\ \max\!\big(0,\ -\tfrac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\,\tfrac{v'_x}{\max(v'_{xx} + v'^2_x,\ 0.05)}\big)\Big)\), with \(v'\) the copy evaluated from the rival's seat (§4). The 0.05 floor is the same safety net as in the 1-D network, and the cap [0, 10] is the same as in the FD solver; how many times they act is counted, and it should be 0. The copy carries no gradient, and it is only differentiated in each seat's own wealth, which only enters the final network: it costs less than 10% of a step and takes less than 1 GB with N = 20.
Measure the change on check states
20,000 fixed states (seed 1): \(t\) uniform on [0, 0.9] and the \(N\) wealths drawn, each on its own, from \(\mathcal N\big(M(t), S(t)^2\big)\), the λ = 0 population. \(t \gt 0.9\) is left out, where the rule near the kink is not reliable even in 1-D. All the numerical checks are measured on these same states (§10).
Stop when the change stops falling
Each short round is 2,200 more training steps, and that alone moves π a little: it is the method's noise, and it is not known in advance. While the rule approaches the equilibrium, Δ falls fast; when only noise is left, it stops falling. That is why the rounds stop as soon as Δ no longer halves, from round 3 on (Δ₂ is needed for the comparison). A fixed threshold could sit below the noise, and the rounds would never stop.
Accept if the last change is below 0.02
It is the mean error in π asked of every check. A mean error of 0.02 in π moves the final mean by at most \(0.06 \times 0.02 \approx 10^{-3}\), the accuracy we ask of the mean. The last Δ is recorded: it says how precisely the final rule is the best response to itself.
No damping; at most 6 rounds
With a loop that contracts this much there are no oscillations to damp. If at the sixth round the change still halves, or the last one is above 0.02, something is wrong: everything is saved and looked at, and it is not taken as an equilibrium.
Save every round
The frozen copy is needed anyway. If something crashes in the middle of the night, at most one round is lost: the script continues from the last saved one when relaunched.

7The networks

src/mfg/pinn/nets.py · SymmetricNet

How many networks there are, what each one receives and gives, and why it has to be symmetric.

One network per N, and one copy per round

For each \(N\) there is a single network, \(v_\theta(t, x, y)\), with \(\theta\) its weights. It belongs to everyone: since everyone uses the same rule, the same network gives my value and, read from each rival's seat (§4), theirs. There is no density network as in 1-D, because there is no Fokker–Planck equation. During a round two copies live side by side:

  • the one being trained: my best response to that round's rivals;
  • a frozen copy of the previous round: it only serves to compute how much each rival invests, and it does not change during the round.

If the optional check C6 is run (§10), one more network is trained, from scratch.

What it receives and what it gives

It receives the time \(t\), my wealth \(x\) and the wealths of my \(N - 1\) rivals, and gives one number, \(v\). Everything else comes from it by automatic differentiation: my value \(V = -e^{v}\) and my rule \(\pi^*\) (§4).

What symmetric means

The rivals have no names. “Rivals with 1.2 and 0.9” is the same situation as “rivals with 0.9 and 1.2”, so the value cannot depend on the order in which they are passed:

\[ v\big(t,\ 1.0,\ (1.2,\ 0.9)\big) = v\big(t,\ 1.0,\ (0.9,\ 1.2)\big). \]

An ordinary network, with a fixed slot for each rival, could give two different numbers, and would have to learn that they are equal. A symmetric network gives the same number by construction. The symmetry is only among the rivals: my wealth has its own slot, because I am not interchangeable with them.

How it is built

  1. Summarise each rival. A small network, the same for all of them (same weights), turns each rival's wealth into 32 numbers: a description of that rival that the network learns to choose.
  2. Average the summaries. The average does not change if the rivals are reordered. This is where the symmetry comes from.
  3. The final network. It receives \(t\), \(x\), the mean of the rivals' wealths \(\bar y\) and the 32 numbers, and gives \(v\). \(\bar y\) also goes in directly because the kink of the final value is exactly at \(x = \bar y\): this way the network does not have to learn where it is.
\[ v_\theta(t, x, y) = \text{final}\Big(t,\ x,\ \bar y,\ \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\text{summary}(y_j)\Big). \]

That this is enough is no accident: any symmetric function can be written this way if the summary has about \(N + 1\) numbers. It is the DeepSets architecture, which Germain, Laurière, Pham and Warin use for symmetric equations in \(N\) agents paper. With 32 we cover all our \(N \le 20\).

Decisions, and why

Mean, not sum
With the mean, the numbers the final network sees have the same scale for any \(N\), and as \(N \to \infty\) they become an average over the population, as in the mean-field. Also, the same weights serve any \(N\): N = 20 can start from the network of N = 10.
Sizes
Summary network: 1 → 32 → 32 → 32, with tanh in the hidden layers and a linear output (2,176 weights). Final network: 35 inputs and 4 layers of 64, like the 1-D V network (TanhNet; 14,849 weights). About 17,000 in total, whatever \(N\) is, against 12,700 in 1-D.
tanh everywhere
The equation uses second derivatives in every wealth, and with ReLU they would be zero. Germain and co-authors found that ReLU approximates single functions better, but it is of no use here.
Inputs rescaled as in 1-D
\(t \mapsto 2t/T - 1\), and each wealth, \(\bar y\) included, becomes \((\text{wealth} - 1)/2\), so that tanh works without saturating. Xavier start and zero biases, as in TanhNet.

A fact from the same paper: in their tests with symmetric functions (N from 10 to 1000), ordinary networks, without enforcing the symmetry, were often enough, and DeepSets was the best of the symmetric ones. We chose the symmetric one for the exactness of the order and to be able to use the same network for every \(N\), not because an ordinary network would surely fail.

8The training points

src/mfg/pinn/nplayer.py · sample_batch

Which situations the network is shown: drawn around the population, of four types, with no box and no edges.

What a point is

A PINN does not see solutions. At each step it receives a batch of situations \((t, x, y)\): a time and the \(N\) wealths. At each one it measures how much the equation fails (§9), and the weights are corrected so that it fails less. Each Adam step uses new situations. The network only learns well where it is shown situations, so choosing where to draw them is choosing where it will be reliable.

They are drawn from four types: realistic groups; groups in which someone is far away, me or a rival; situations near the end with my wealth next to the rivals' mean, where the rule changes fastest; and the final time, where the final condition is imposed. The figure shows the first three.

Why not a box as in 1-D

In 1-D the network lived on the rectangle \([0, 1]\times[-1, 3]\), with known values on the edges. In \(N\) dimensions the box \([-1, 3]^N\) has \(2N\) faces with no known values, and almost all of its volume is impossible situations, such as everyone being at \(-1\) at the same time.

Nor does it work to draw each wealth widely on its own. With 19 rivals drawn from a normal with standard deviation 0.75, the probability that all 19 fall within 0.3 of the centre, as in a real group, is \(0.31^{19} \approx 2\cdot10^{-10}\): the network would never see a realistic group. That is why the centre and the width of the group are drawn first, and then each wealth.

The exact recipe

  1. Type A, a realistic group: 2,000 points per step. \(t\) uniform on \([0, T]\). Centre \(c = M(t) + \mathcal U[-0.3, 0.3]\) and width \(s = S(t)\cdot\mathcal U[0.5, 1.5]\). The \(N\) wealths, mine included, drawn from \(\mathcal N(c, s^2)\).
  2. Type B, someone far away: 2,000 points per step. A type A point in which a single wealth is replaced by a draw from \(\mathcal U[-1.5, 3.5]\). Mine with probability ½; otherwise a random rival's.
  3. Kink: 1,000 points per step. \(t\) uniform on \([T - 0.1, T]\), rivals as in A, and me at \(x = \bar y + \mathcal U[-0.3, 0.3]\). It is where the rule changes fastest, like the extra 1-D points near \((T, \bar m)\).
  4. Final: 1,000 points per step. \(t = T\), half of type A and half of type B.

\(M(t)\) and \(S(t)\) are the mean and the standard deviation of the λ = 0 population (population_mean and the square root of population_var): \(S\) goes from 0.20 at \(t = 0\) to 0.36 at \(T\). The points are new at each Adam step; L-BFGS polishes on one fixed draw, as in 1-D.

Why each type

Random centre and width
At the end, the real λ = 2 group has a width of about 0.77·S(T) (0.278 against 0.363) and its centre a little below \(M(T)\) (1.065 against 1.110), within what is drawn. Shifting the centre gives situations of being ahead of or behind the group, which the ranking needs.
Me far away
To read my rule at any wealth in \([0, 2.2]\) and beyond, even if the group is far away.
A rival far away
When I am far away, each rival sees me as their far rival. To read their rule from their seat, the network must have seen that situation. With the two cases of B, every situation that is read is among the trained ones.
No edges
There is no box, so there are no faces to fix. The ends of the uniform draws, \(-1.5\) and \(3.5\), are far from where we read results. In the time left, a wealth moves by about \(\sigma\pi\sqrt{T - t} \approx 0.2 \times 1.5 \times 1 = 0.3\). The probability of travelling from 2.2 to 3.5, more than 4 standard deviations, is \(2\Phi(-1.3/0.3) \approx 1.5\cdot10^{-5}\). What the network does at the ends does not reach \([0, 2.2]\).

A rule for reading results

Only situations among the trained ones are read. What the network says about situations nobody reaches, such as two investors very far from the centre at the same time and on opposite sides, is neither used nor compared. There are two tools for this:

  • The check states (§6): 20,000 fixed situations, drawn from the population. The change between rounds and all the numerical checks are measured there, including the optional N = 2 comparison with FD.
  • To plot my rule against my wealth, the rivals are placed at the quantiles of the population at that time, \(y_j = \bar y + s_t\,\Phi^{-1}\!\big((j - \tfrac12)/(N-1)\big)\). This way the group has a realistic width. Everyone at the same point (zero width) was never trained, so it is not used.

9The loss, part by part

src/mfg/pinn/nplayer.py · nplayer_loss

Three parts, each a mean of squares, with coefficient 1. It is the 1-D loss without the edges and with the rivals inside the equation.

\[ L(\theta) = L_{\text{eq}} + L_T + L_{\text{slopes}}. \]

1 · The equation, \(L_{\text{eq}}\)

\[ L_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{\text{points}} R^2, \qquad R = \frac{v_t + r x v_x + \sum_j\Big[\big(r y_j + \pi_j(\mu - r)\big)v_{y_j} + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\pi_j^2\big(v_{y_jy_j} + v_{y_j}^2\big)\Big]}{\kappa^2/2} - \frac{v_x^2}{\max\big(v_{xx} + v_x^2,\ 0.05\big)}. \]
  • Points: those of types A and B and the kink points (5,000 per step).
  • What it measures: how much the HJB of section 4 fails.
  • Why this way: it is divided by \(\kappa^2/2\) for the same reason as in 1-D, so that its terms are of size one. The \(\pi_j\) come from the frozen copy (§6) and carry no gradient: during the round they are data.

2 · The final value, \(L_T\)

\[ L_T = \frac{1}{n_T}\sum_{\text{final points}} \Big(v_\theta(T, x, y) - \log\!\big(e^{-\gamma x} + \tfrac{\lambda_N}{2}\big((\bar y - x)^+\big)^2\big)\Big)^2. \]
  • Points: the 1,000 at \(t = T\).
  • What it measures: whether the network gives exactly the final utility.
  • Why: it is the only thing that pins down the solution, because the HJB runs backward from \(T\).

3 · My own slopes at the end, \(L_{\text{slopes}}\)

\[ L_{\text{slopes}} = \frac{1}{n'}\sum_{|x - \bar y| \gt 0.05}\Big[\big(v_x - g_1\big)^2 + \big(v_{xx} - g_2\big)^2\Big], \qquad g_1 = \frac{U_x}{U},\quad g_2 = \frac{U_{xx}}{U} - g_1^2. \]
  • Points: the final ones that are not within 0.05 of the rivals' mean.
  • What it measures: whether at \(T\) the network has the right slope and curvature in my wealth. \(U\) is the utility with \(\lambda_N\) and \(\bar y\); the targets are terminal_targets(x, ybar, p.with_(lam=lam_N)).
  • Why: \(\pi^*\) depends on \(v_{xx}\), which the value alone does not pin down well (we learnt that in 1-D). Near \(\bar y\), \(U\) has a kink and \(v_{xx}\) jumps, so that band is not compared.
  • Why only mine: the rule only uses the derivatives in my wealth, and each rival's slope is \(N - 1\) times smaller than mine: \(\lambda(\bar x - x)/N\) against \(\lambda_N(\bar y - x)\).

How the derivatives are computed

All by automatic differentiation. One backward pass gives \(v_t\), \(v_x\) and all the \(v_{y_j}\). Then one more pass for \(v_{xx}\) and one per rival for each \(v_{y_jy_j}\): \(N\) extra passes, and each one goes through the \(N - 1\) rivals. This is the cost that grows with \(N\).

How a round is trained

With the settings of the 1-D V network. Round 1: 10,000 Adam steps with the step size falling from 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴, plus 500 of L-BFGS. Later rounds: 2,000 of Adam (3·10⁻⁴ → 10⁻⁴) plus 200 of L-BFGS. All in float32 and on the GPU.

L-BFGS polishes on one fixed batch of points, and in many dimensions it could fit that batch and get worse away from it: with N = 20 the network has about 17,000 weights, and the batch imposes about 8,000 conditions on it. That is why, before L-BFGS, a copy of the weights is kept, and afterwards the loss of both networks is computed on a new batch, the same for both. The one with the smaller loss is kept.

10The checks

The aim is to see how the problem changes with N, not to certify every number. The minimum is cheap and says whether the code solves the right equation; the rest is optional.

The minimum

  1. Tests without training, in seconds. The network is replaced by exact formulas. The exact solution of C2 (below) must give zero residual, and its rule at every seat. The seat example of §4 must come out: with the made-up function \(v = -\text{own} + 0.1\cdot\text{own}\cdot\bar y\), the rival who has 3 invests \(1.5/(1 - 0.1\cdot 7/3) = 1.957\), and a badly built seat would give 2.0 or 2.143. Also, reordering the rivals does not change the network's output, and \(\lambda_N = 0.5\) with N = 2.
  2. C2 trained with N = 5, about 10 minutes. Original utility with \(\eta = 0.5\): \(\pi(0) = 1.4703/(1 - 0.5/5) = 1.6337\). It tests the rivals' terms and the rounds at the same time: the exact rule does not change after round 1, so the rounds must stop right away, at round 3 or 4. It passes with the 1-D targets, on the check states: mean error in \(\pi\) below 0.02, maximum below 0.07, and \(V\) within 0.5%.
  3. C3 and the change between rounds, for free. At \(t = T\), the rule must be pi_terminal(x, ybar, p.with_(lam=lam_N)) outside the kink band. And the last Δ of each N (§6) says how precisely the final rule is the best response to itself.

Without these three things, an error in the seats or in the rivals' terms would give the equilibrium of another game, with believable figures. The Monte Carlo of games is not a check: it is how we see how the group ends up (§4), so it always runs.

The exact solution of C2

With \(U = -\exp\!\big(-\gamma(x - \eta\bar x)\big)\), and everyone with the constant rule \(\pi(t) = \dfrac{(\mu - r)e^{-r(T-t)}}{\gamma(1 - \eta/N)\sigma^2}\):

\[ v(t, x, y) = -\gamma e^{r(T-t)}\Big[\Big(1 - \frac{\eta}{N}\Big)x - \frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{j} y_j\Big] + \kappa^2 k_N\,(T - t), \qquad k_N = -\frac12 + (N - 1)\Big[\frac{\eta}{N - \eta} + \frac12\Big(\frac{\eta}{N - \eta}\Big)^2\Big]. \]

Check it by hand

Put \(v\) into the equation of §4. Since \(v\) is linear in the wealths, \(v_{xx} = v_{y_jy_j} = 0\), and the terms with \(x\) and with each \(y_j\) cancel. A constant is left, which fixes \(k_N\). The rule comes from \(\pi^* = -\tfrac{\mu - r}{\sigma^2}\,\tfrac{v_x}{v_x^2}\).

With N = 5: \(\eta/(N - \eta) = 1/9\), \(k_5 = -\tfrac12 + 4\big(\tfrac19 + \tfrac1{162}\big) = -\tfrac{5}{162}\), and \(\kappa^2 k_5 = -0.00278\). With \(\eta = 0\), \(k_N = -\tfrac12\), which is C1.

What will be said about the results

  • They are what the network finds, validated on the exact cases. Without the optional checks, the λ = 2 case has no external reference, so they are reported as trends.
  • The network only holds in realistic situations (§8).
  • It is an equilibrium to the method's precision: below the training noise it cannot be told apart from something very close to one.
  • With N = 10 and 20, the difference from the mean-field is a few thousandths, of the order of our precision: there the mean-field already holds.
Extra checks optional

They give more confidence in the numbers, at the cost of computing time or of Deepak's work. With them, the λ = 2 case would have an external reference at N = 2 and an equilibrium certificate at N = 5 and 10.

  1. C1 · No penalty (λ = 0), trained. Exact: \(v = -\gamma e^{r(T-t)}x - \tfrac12\kappa^2(T - t)\), independent of the rivals, and \(\pi = 1.47\) at \(t = 0\). With N = 5, about 5 minutes.
  2. C4 · N = 2 against finite differences in 2-D. Deepak would compute it (specification below). It is compared on the N = 2 check states, and on slices with my wealth in [0, 2.2] and the rival at 0.8, 1.0 and 1.2, which are trained situations.
  3. C5 · Monte Carlo of the value. Games are played with the network's rule from wealths drawn from \(\mathcal N(1, 0.2^2)\). The mean of \(U(X^1_T, \bar X_T)\) must equal the mean of \(V(0, X^1_0, \text{rivals}_0)\) on the same draws, within 3 standard errors plus 0.5%.
  4. C6 · Best response from scratch. With N = 5 and 10, the best response against the final rivals is trained from scratch, and the mean of \(|\pi_{\text{new}} - \pi_{\text{final}}|\) is measured on the check states. If it is less than twice the difference between two seeds, it is an equilibrium to the method's precision. The comparison is in π and not in utility, because utility barely changes near the best rule: an error of 0.1 in π costs about \(\tfrac12\gamma\sigma^2\cdot 0.1^2 = 2\cdot10^{-4}\) sure dollars.
  5. C7 · Towards the mean-field. With the rivals at the quantiles of the mean-field population, the rule for large N must approach the 1-D one. It is more a result than a test.
  6. Two seeds with N = 3, 5 and 10. How much π changes between them is the real precision, as in 1-D, and it gives error bars to the figures.

The finite-difference reference for N = 2 (for Deepak)

  • Unknown: \(V(t, x, y)\) on \([0, 1]\times[-1.5, 3.5]^2\), with λ = 2 (so \(\lambda_2 = 0.5\)).
  • Equation: the one of §4 with one rival. The rival's position is the previous round's rule with the roles swapped, \(\pi_{\text{rival}}(t, x, y) = \pi(t, y, x)\); on a symmetric grid it is the transpose.
  • Rounds: as in §6, with Merton at the start, until the largest change of π on \([0, 2.2]^2\) and \(t \le 0.9\) falls below 10⁻⁶.
  • Edges: your choice. Condition to accept them: if the box grows by 0.5 on each side, π on \([0, 2.2]^2\) does not change by more than 10⁻³.
  • Suggested grid: 400 × 400 nodes and 400 time steps, implicit, with policy iteration as in 1-D; check with a finer one.
  • Tests: λ = 0 gives Merton; the original utility (\(\eta = 0.5\)) gives \(\pi(0) = 1.96\).
  • Interface: value(t, x, y) and position(t, x, y) with NumPy, like the network's (NPlayerSolution in src/mfg/pinn/nplayer.py).
