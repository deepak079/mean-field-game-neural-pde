Where the HJB and the Fokker–Planck equations come from

The two equations of the project come from one and the same calculation: how much a quantity changes, on average, when wealth moves with a predictable part and a part that is luck. The HJB applies that calculation to the value of one investor and looks for the best strategy. The Fokker–Planck applies it to the whole population and follows how the money is spread. If they look arbitrary, it is because the problem statement gives the result and skips the calculation. Here is the calculation, term by term, with charts you can move.

1The whole map

There are two unknowns. \(V(t,x)\) says how well an investor who has wealth \(x\) at time \(t\) can do. \(\cM{m}(t,x)\) says how the wealth of the whole population is spread. Each one has its own equation, and each equation needs a piece of data that the other one produces.

The rest of the guide goes through this drawing from top to bottom: first the rule at the top, then each box, and at the end the two arrows that join them.

2Minimal vocabulary

The equations use few symbols. This is what each one means in this problem.

SymbolNameWhat it means here
\(\cT{\partial_t V}\)time derivativeHow much \(V\) changes if an instant goes by and your wealth does not move.
\(\partial_x V\)slopeHow much \(V\) goes up if you have one more dollar. It is what an extra dollar is worth.
\(\cR{\partial_{xx} V}\)curvatureHow that value of the extra dollar changes. If it is negative, each extra dollar is worth a little less than the previous one. That is risk aversion.
\(\mathbb E[\,\cdot\,]\)expectationThe average if you repeated the same situation a huge number of times, each time with different luck.
\(\cR{dW_t}\)Brownian shockA noise with mean 0 and variance \(dt\). Its typical size is \(\sqrt{dt}\).

\(\cM{m}(t,x)\)densityThe histogram of the population: \(m\,dx\) is the fraction of investors with wealth between \(x\) and \(x+dx\). Its total area is 1.
\(\cM{\bar m_t}=\int x\,m\,dx\)meanThe average wealth of the population at time \(t\).
\((y)^+\)positive part\(\max(y,0)\). In the penalty, only ending below the mean counts.

The fact that changes everything

In a short interval \(dt\), the predictable part moves you by something of order \(dt\). Luck moves you by something of order \(\sqrt{dt}\), which is much larger: with \(dt = 0.01\), \(\sqrt{dt} = 0.1\), ten times more. That is why, when there is randomness, squared terms cannot be thrown away as in ordinary calculus: \((dW)^2\) is of order \(dt\), the same as the drift. This is where the term with \(\tfrac12\) that appears in both equations comes from.

3The two parts of the motion

The wealth of an investor follows equation (1) of the problem statement. Each part has its colour:

\[ dX_t \;=\; \underbrace{\cB{\big(rX_t+\cP{\pi_t}(\mu-r)\big)}}_{\text{the predictable part}}\,dt \;+\; \underbrace{\cR{\sigma\,\cP{\pi_t}\,dW_t}}_{\text{luck}} \tag{1} \]

Everything that happens in the model depends on two numbers, which are worth naming right away:

\[ \cB{b(t,x)} = rx+\cP\pi(\mu-r), \qquad \cR{D(t,x)} = \sigma^2\cP\pi^2 . \]
  • \(\cB{b}\) is the drift: how much you expect to gain per unit of time. \(rx\) is the bank interest on all your money and \(\pi(\mu-r)\) is the extra for having \(\pi\) dollars in the stock.
  • \(\cR{D}\) is the variance per unit of time: how hard luck shakes you. In an interval \(dt\), your wealth moves away from the predictable path by something of typical size \(\sqrt{D\,dt} = \sigma\pi\sqrt{dt}\).

With the numbers of the project

\(\mu - r = 0.06\) and \(\sigma = 0.2\). Each dollar in the stock adds 6 cents a year on average and an uncertainty of ±20 cents (one standard deviation). With \(\pi = 1.5\) dollars: +9 cents expected and ±30 cents of luck. Careful: \(\pi\) is in dollars, not a percentage of wealth.

The key asymmetry. \(\pi\) enters \(\cB{b}\) linearly and \(\cR{D}\) squared. Doubling the investment doubles the expected gain but multiplies the variance by four. That is why there is an optimal amount: the first dollars in the stock pay off, and there comes a point where the risk each dollar adds weighs more than what it brings. That point is \(\cP{\pi^*}\), and section 6 computes it.

4The core calculation: Taylor with luck

Take any function \(f(t,x)\): the utility, the value \(V\), anything. If your wealth is \(x\) at time \(t\), how much does \(f\) change on average over an instant \(dt\)? With Taylor:

\[ f(t+dt,\;x+dX) \;\approx\; f \;+\; \cT{\partial_t f\,dt} \;+\; \partial_x f\,dX \;+\; \tfrac12\,\partial_{xx}f\,(dX)^2 . \]

Now take the average. On average, \(dX\) is \(\cB{b}\,dt\), because luck has mean zero. And \((dX)^2\) is on average \(\cR{D}\,dt\): it is the square of luck, which does not cancel because a square is never negative.

\[ \mathbb E[dX] = \cB{b}\,dt, \qquad \mathbb E\big[(dX)^2\big] \approx \cR{D}\,dt . \]

The core calculation (Itô's lemma, the everyday version)

\[ \mathbb E[df] \;\approx\; \Big(\,\cT{\partial_t f} \;+\; \cB{b\,\partial_x f} \;+\; \cR{\tfrac12\, D\,\partial_{xx} f}\,\Big)\,dt \]
\(\cT{\partial_t f}\)
time

What \(f\) changes only because the clock runs.

\(\cB{b\,\partial_x f}\)
the predictable part

How much you move on average (\(b\)) times how much \(f\) goes up for each unit you move (the slope).

\(\cR{\tfrac12 D\,\partial_{xx} f}\)
luck

The variance (\(D\)) times the curvature of \(f\), times \(\tfrac12\). If \(f\) is concave (bends downwards), this term is negative: luck takes value away on average even though its mean is zero.

Detail: why \((dX)^2\approx D\,dt\)

\((dX)^2 = b^2\,dt^2 + 2b\sigma\pi\,dt\,dW + \sigma^2\pi^2\,(dW)^2\). The first two terms are of size \(dt^2\) and \(dt^{3/2}\), much smaller than \(dt\), and are dropped. The third is not: \((dW)^2\) is \(dt\) on average. What is left is \(\sigma^2\pi^2\,dt = D\,dt\).

Why does luck take value away if its mean is zero? The chart shows it. From \(x_0 = 1\), luck takes you to \(x_0 + s\) or to \(x_0 - s\) with the same probability. The average utility at those two points sits at the middle of the chord that joins them. Since the curve bends downwards, that middle lies below \(U(x_0)\). Losing \(s\) hurts more than gaining \(s\) pleases.

Why this calculation is the key

It is the whole "physics" of the problem. The HJB is this calculation applied to the value \(V\), plus the question of which \(\pi\) is best. The Fokker–Planck is this same calculation applied to the whole population at once. If you understand its three terms, you already know where every term of the two equations comes from.

5HJB: the best plan, backwards

What V is

\(V(t,x)\) is the best average final score that an investor who has wealth \(x\) at time \(t\) can reach, playing as well as possible from now on. The "score" is the utility \(U\) of the problem statement, and the crowd comes in through \(\cM{\bar m_T}\). At the end there is nothing left to decide, so the score is just the utility:

\[ V(T,x) \;=\; U(x,\cM{\bar m_T}) \;=\; -e^{-\gamma x} \;-\; \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\Big(\big(\cM{\bar m_T}-x\big)^+\Big)^2 . \]

For earlier times \(V\) is not known: it is what has to be computed. The HJB is the rule that computes it.

Bellman's principle

The idea is common sense: the best plan from now on is to take the best decision at this instant and, from wherever you land, carry on with the best plan. As a formula:

\[ V(t,x) \;=\; \max_{\cP{\pi}}\ \mathbb E\Big[\,V\big(t+dt,\;X_{t+dt}\big)\Big] . \]

It works like a GPS. If the fastest route from New York to Boston goes through Providence, its stretch from Providence to Boston has to be the fastest route from Providence to Boston. If it were not, you would change that stretch and arrive sooner.

From Bellman to the HJB in two lines

Inside the average there is \(V\) one instant later. The core calculation, with \(f = V\), says what that average is:

\[ \mathbb E\big[V(t+dt,X_{t+dt})\big] \;\approx\; V(t,x) \;+\; \Big(\cT{\partial_t V} + \cB{b\,\partial_x V} + \cR{\tfrac12 D\,\partial_{xx}V}\Big)\,dt . \]

Substitute it into Bellman. \(V(t,x)\) appears on both sides and cancels. Divide by \(dt\). And since \(\partial_t V\) does not depend on \(\pi\), it comes out of the maximum. Writing \(b\) and \(D\) out in full:

The HJB, equation (3) of the problem statement

\[ \cT{\partial_t V} \;+\; \sup_{\cP\pi}\Big\{\,\cB{\big(rx+\cP\pi(\mu-r)\big)\,\partial_x V} \;+\; \cR{\tfrac12\sigma^2\cP\pi^2\,\partial_{xx}V}\,\Big\} \;=\; 0 \tag{3} \]

Nothing in it is arbitrary: it is Bellman plus Taylor with luck. We will call what is inside the sup the bracket.

Why "= 0"

There are two cases, and together they give the equals-zero:

  • With any \(\pi\), the average of \(V\) one instant later cannot exceed \(V\) now. \(V\) is already the best possible: if some strategy made you expect more, \(V\) would not be the best. So \(\partial_t V\) plus the bracket is \(\le 0\) for every \(\pi\).
  • With the best \(\pi\), the average does not change. Following the best plan, your "best possible score" only moves through surprises of luck, and those surprises have mean zero. So with the best \(\pi\) the sum is exactly 0.

Each term

\(\cT{\partial_t V}\)
time

How much \(V\) changes only because time passes, with the same wealth. Here it is negative: with the same money, later is worse, because you have less time left to collect the risk premium.

\(\cB{rx\,\partial_x V}\)
the predictable part

The bank interest (\(rx\) dollars a year) times what each dollar is worth.

\(\cB{\cP\pi(\mu-r)\,\partial_x V}\)
the predictable part

The extra expected gain from having \(\pi\) dollars in the stock, times what each dollar is worth. It is the benefit of taking risk.

\(\cR{\tfrac12\sigma^2\cP\pi^2\,\partial_{xx}V}\)
luck

The variance of your wealth times the curvature of \(V\). It is the sag of the chord in the previous chart: the cost of risk. It is negative because \(\partial_{xx}V<0\).

\(\sup_{\cP\pi}\{\dots\}\)
the decision

The investor chooses the \(\pi\) that makes the bracket as large as possible. It is the only place where anyone decides anything.

How to read it

Along the best strategy, what time takes away (\(\cT{\partial_t V}<0\)) is exactly made up by what the best move gives you on average: interest, plus premium, minus the cost of risk.

Why it runs backwards. \(V\) is known only at the end. The equation gives \(\partial_t V\) from the shape of \(V\) in \(x\) at that same instant. From \(V(T,\cdot)\) you compute \(V\) a little earlier; from that, a little earlier still; and so on down to \(t = 0\). It is watching the film in reverse.

About the \(F\) of the original statement. There the bracket carried a term \(F(x,m_t)\) for rewards or punishments along the way. In this problem all the interaction is in the final score, so \(F = 0\).

6Why π* is that formula

Inside the bracket there is only one thing the investor controls: \(\pi\). If you separate what depends on \(\pi\), the bracket is a parabola:

\[ \underbrace{\cB{rx\,\partial_x V}}_{\text{does not depend on }\pi} \;+\; \cP\pi\,\underbrace{\cB{(\mu-r)\,\partial_x V}}_{A\;>\;0} \;-\; \cP\pi^2\,\underbrace{\cR{\tfrac12\sigma^2\,(-\partial_{xx}V)}}_{B\;>\;0} \]

A gain that grows in a straight line minus a cost that grows with the square. Since \(B > 0\), the parabola opens downwards and has a top. The top is where the slope is zero:

The optimal strategy, formula (4)

\[ A - 2B\,\cP\pi = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \cP{\pi^*} \;=\; \frac{A}{2B} \;=\; -\,\frac{(\mu-r)\,\partial_x V}{\sigma^2\,\partial_{xx} V} \tag{4} \]

There is no other possibility: it is the only point where the parabola stops rising. Read in words, it says marginal gain = marginal cost:

\[ \underbrace{\cB{(\mu-r)\,\partial_x V}}_{\text{what one more dollar brings}} \;=\; \underbrace{\cR{\sigma^2\,\cP\pi\,(-\partial_{xx}V)}}_{\text{what one more dollar costs}} \]

One more dollar in the stock always brings the same. But its cost grows with what you have already invested, because the variance grows like \(\pi^2\). You keep adding dollars while they bring more than they cost, and you stop just when they break even.

The two parts of the formula

\[ \cP{\pi^*} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma^2}}_{\text{market offer}\,=\,1.5} \;\times\; \underbrace{\Big(-\frac{\partial_x V}{\partial_{xx} V}\Big)}_{\text{your risk tolerance}} \]
  • \((\mu-r)/\sigma^2 = 0.06/0.04 = 1.5\) is the same for everyone: what the market pays for each unit of variance you accept.
  • \(-\partial_x V/\partial_{xx}V\) is personal and measured in dollars: how much risk you can bear. If \(V\) bends a lot compared with its slope, you bear little.
  • \(\partial_{xx}V<0\) is needed. If \(V\) bent upwards, the parabola would open upwards and more \(\pi\) would always be better: you would bet infinitely. That is why, in the PINN, if the network loses concavity, \(\pi^*\) blows up (section 9, step 1).

The case without interaction: Merton

With \(\lambda = 0\) the solution of the HJB is known:

\[ V(t,x) \;=\; -\exp\!\Big(-\gamma\,e^{r(T-t)}\,x \;-\; \tfrac12\kappa^2\,(T-t)\Big), \qquad \kappa=\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma}=0.3 . \]

Differentiating twice in \(x\) and dividing:

\[ \partial_x V = -\gamma e^{r(T-t)}\,V,\qquad \partial_{xx}V = \gamma^2 e^{2r(T-t)}\,V \quad\Longrightarrow\quad -\frac{\partial_x V}{\partial_{xx}V} = \frac{e^{-r(T-t)}}{\gamma} \] \[ \cP{\pi^*(t)} \;=\; \frac{(\mu-r)\,e^{-r(T-t)}}{\gamma\,\sigma^2} \;\approx\; 1.47 \text{ at } t=0, \qquad 1.5 \text{ at } t=T . \]
  • The tolerance does not depend on \(x\). Everyone invests the same dollars, whatever their wealth. It is the property of the exponential utility: constant absolute risk aversion.
  • The factor \(e^{-r(T-t)}\). A dollar you win or lose today grows with interest into \(e^{r(T-t)}\) dollars at the end. Risking one dollar today is like risking a bit more than one dollar at \(T\), so at the start one invests a little less.

Putting π* back into the HJB

The maximum of the parabola is \(A^2/(4B)\). Substituting it, the HJB no longer has \(\pi\):

\[ \cT{\partial_t V} \;+\; \cB{rx\,\partial_x V} \;-\; \frac{(\mu-r)^2\,(\partial_x V)^2}{2\sigma^2\,\partial_{xx}V} \;=\; 0 . \]

This is the form the PINN uses. It is nonlinear because \(\partial_{xx}V\) is in a denominator. The last term is what investing well is worth: the gain minus the cost of risk at the top.

Check with Merton. Call \(c = \gamma e^{r(T-t)}\) and \(E = e^{-c x - \frac12\kappa^2(T-t)} > 0\), so that \(V = -E\). Each term is:

TermValueWhere it comes from
\(\cT{\partial_t V}\)\(-E\,\big(r c\,x + \tfrac12\kappa^2\big)\)the clock: interest and time to invest are lost
\(\cB{rx\,\partial_x V}\)\(+E\,r c\,x\)interest makes up part of it
\(-\dfrac{(\mu-r)^2(\partial_x V)^2}{2\sigma^2\partial_{xx}V}\)\(+E\,\tfrac12\kappa^2\)the risk premium makes up the rest

The factor \(e^{-\frac12\kappa^2(T-t)}\) in \(V\) is what the time left to invest is worth: \(\tfrac12\kappa^2 = 0.045\) per year. The more time is left, the bigger the reward; that is why \(V\) falls over time and \(\cT{\partial_t V}<0\).

7Fokker–Planck: the crowd, forwards

The HJB looks at one single investor. The Fokker–Planck looks at all of them at once: it does not follow anyone in particular, but how the spread of wealth changes. It is built in four steps, which are the parts of this section:

  1. What \(m\) is. A histogram of the population taken to the limit.
  2. The bookkeeping. The people in a small box only change because they cross its walls. That gives \(\partial_t m = -\partial_x J\), and it is pure bookkeeping.
  3. What the flow \(J\) is. Here the model comes in: the predictable part pushes everyone to one side and luck spreads them out.
  4. Putting it together. Out comes equation (5), and from it the mean and the width of the population.

7.1 What m is: from histogram to density

Imagine \(N\) investors. Divide the wealth axis into small boxes of width \(h\) and count how many fall in each: \(n_i\) in box \(i\). That count depends on two things that have nothing to do with how wealth is spread:

  • It depends on \(N\). With twice as many investors, twice as many people in each box. It is fixed by dividing by \(N\): \(n_i/N\) is the fraction of the population in the box.
  • It depends on \(h\). A box twice as wide collects twice as many people. It is fixed by also dividing by \(h\): \(n_i/(N h)\) is the fraction per unit of wealth.

This last number no longer depends on how many investors there are or on the width of the boxes. With ever finer boxes and a huge number of investors, the values \(n_i/(Nh)\) draw a smooth curve. That curve is the density:

\[ \cM{m}(t,x) \;\approx\; \frac{\text{investors with wealth between } x \text{ and } x+h \text{ at time } t}{N\cdot h} . \]

It is the same idea as the population density on a map, in people per km². To know how many people live in an area, you multiply the density by the surface. Here, to know what fraction of investors has wealth between \(a\) and \(b\), you compute the area under \(m\) between \(a\) and \(b\):

\[ \text{fraction with wealth between } a \text{ and } b \;=\; \int_a^b \cM{m}(t,x)\,dx, \qquad\quad \int \cM{m}(t,x)\,dx \;=\; 1 . \]

A concrete value

At the start \(m(0,\cdot) = \mathcal N(1,\,0.2^2)\), and its height at \(x = 1\) is \(m(0,1)\approx 1.99\). That does not mean "199 %". It means that in a small box of width 0.01 around 1 there are roughly \(1.99\times 0.01\approx 2\%\) of the investors. \(m\) is measured in "fraction of the population per dollar of wealth".

Population or probability, it is the same. Since each investor has their own luck and there are infinitely many, the fraction of the population with wealth near \(x\) equals the probability that any given investor has wealth near \(x\). That is why \(m\) is called the "distribution" of wealth: it can be read both ways.

7.2 The bookkeeping: what comes in minus what goes out

Look at a single box, the one for wealth between \(x-\tfrac h2\) and \(x+\tfrac h2\). The number of people inside can only change for one reason: someone crosses one of its two walls. Nobody is born or disappears, and nobody jumps all at once to a distant box, because in a short instant wealth only moves a little. So every change in the box is people moving to a neighbouring box or arriving from it.

To keep that account a new word is needed. The flow \(J(t,x)\) at a point \(x\) is how many people cross that point per unit of time, counting those going right as positive and those going left as negative, and measured as a fraction of the population:

\[ J(t,x) \;=\; \frac{\big(\text{fraction crossing } x \text{ to the right}\big) \;-\; \big(\text{fraction crossing } x \text{ to the left}\big)}{\text{time}} . \]

It is like the counter at a toll booth that adds the cars going one way and subtracts those going the other way. \(J > 0\) means that, on net, people move towards higher wealth.

Now the account of the box over an instant \(dt\). Through its left wall, at \(x-\tfrac h2\), a net \(J\big(t,x-\tfrac h2\big)\,dt\) comes in. Through its right wall, at \(x+\tfrac h2\), a net \(J\big(t,x+\tfrac h2\big)\,dt\) goes out. And the fraction of the population inside is density times width, \(\cM{m}\cdot h\). The account says:

\[ \underbrace{\big[\,m(t+dt,x) - m(t,x)\,\big]\cdot h}_{\text{change in what is in the box}} \;=\; \underbrace{J\big(t,x-\tfrac h2\big)\,dt}_{\text{comes in on the left}} \;-\; \underbrace{J\big(t,x+\tfrac h2\big)\,dt}_{\text{goes out on the right}} . \]

Divide both sides by \(h\cdot dt\):

\[ \frac{m(t+dt,x)-m(t,x)}{dt} \;=\; -\,\frac{J\big(t,x+\tfrac h2\big) - J\big(t,x-\tfrac h2\big)}{h} . \]

On the left there is a "change in \(m\) over time": with a small \(dt\) it is \(\partial_t m\). On the right there is a "change in \(J\) over distance": with a small \(h\) it is \(\partial_x J\). What is left is:

Conservation law: the bookkeeping of an infinitely narrow box

\[ \cT{\partial_t m} \;=\; -\,\partial_x J \]

So there is nothing mysterious about \(\partial_x J\): it is how much more goes out through the right wall than comes in through the left one, per unit of width. If more goes out than comes in, \(\partial_x J > 0\) and the box empties (\(\partial_t m < 0\)). If more comes in than goes out, it fills up. The minus sign is exactly that.

With numbers

A box around \(x = 1\) with \(h = 0.1\). Suppose that a net \(J = 0.30\) per year crosses its left wall (30 % of the population per year) and \(J = 0.34\) per year its right wall. In \(dt = 0.01\) years, 0.30 % of the population comes in and 0.34 % goes out: the box loses 0.04 %. Spread over its width of 0.1, the density falls by \(0.0004/0.1 = 0.004\). Per year, \(\partial_t m = -0.4\), which is exactly \(-(0.34-0.30)/0.1\).

The units match: \(m\) is a fraction per dollar and \(J\) a fraction per year, so \(\partial_t m\) and \(\partial_x J\) are both "fraction per dollar per year".

This equation is bookkeeping, not physics. It holds for anything that is conserved: cars on a motorway, water in a pipe, money between accounts. What is specific to this problem is what \(J\) is, and that comes in parts 7.3 and 7.4.

The total is conserved. If you add up the account of all the boxes, each inner wall appears twice, once as an exit from one box and once as an entry to its neighbour, and cancels out. Only the two outer walls are left. If nobody crosses them, the total area of \(m\) stays at 1 forever. That is why the proposal asks for \(m = 0\) at the edges and to check that the total mass stays close to 1.

7.3 What the flow is: the predictable part

The bookkeeping says nothing about the problem until we know what \(J\) is. Start with the case without luck: everyone near \(x\) moves at the same speed \(\cB{b}\), in dollars per year. In an instant \(dt\) each one moves forward by \(\cB{b}\,dt\). Who crosses the wall at \(x\)? Exactly those who were, on its left, less than \(\cB{b}\,dt\) away from it.

How many are they? Density times the width of the strip: \(\cM{m}\cdot\cB{b}\,dt\). Dividing by \(dt\):

The flow of the predictable part

\[ J_{\text{predictable}} \;=\; \cB{b}\,\cM{m} \]

It is the motorway rule: cars per hour passing a point = cars per kilometre × kilometres per hour. Put into the bookkeeping, and expanding the derivative of the product:

\[ \cT{\partial_t m} \;=\; -\,\partial_x\big(\cB{b}\,m\big) \;=\; -\,\cB{b}\,\partial_x m \;-\; m\,\partial_x \cB{b} . \]
  • \(-b\,\partial_x m\) shifts the shape. If everyone moves at the same speed, the histogram moves without changing shape. For example, with a constant \(b\), \(m(t,x) = m_0(x - bt)\) satisfies the equation: the initial shape, shifted \(bt\) to the right.
  • \(-m\,\partial_x b\) stretches or squeezes. If those on the right move faster than those on the left, they spread apart and the density falls. Here \(b = rx + \pi(\mu-r)\), so \(\partial_x b = r\): interest gives more dollars to whoever has more, and the population stretches by a factor \(e^{rt}\). That is why, without luck, the standard deviation goes from 0.200 to \(0.200\,e^{0.02} = 0.204\) in a year.

7.4 What the flow is: the luck part

Luck pushes each person to one side at random, with no preferred direction. It looks as if there can be no net flow this way, but there is. On both sides of a wall people are shaking, and each one crosses to the right with the same probability as to the left. If there are more people on the left side, more people cross from left to right than the other way round. The net flow goes from where there are more people to where there are fewer, like a drop of ink spreading in water. You can see it in the bookkeeping chart: with luck on, hundreds of people cross each wall in each direction, and the flow is the small difference.

To put numbers on it, go back to the small boxes of width \(h\). At each instant \(dt\), a fraction \(q_j\) of the people in box \(j\) jumps to a neighbouring box, half to each side. What is \(q_j\)? Whatever the model says: in it, luck moves each person's wealth with variance \(\cR{D_j}\,dt\). Whoever jumps moves \(\pm h\), so the variance of the movement is \(q_j\,h^2\). Setting the two equal, \(q_j = \cR{D_j}\,dt/h^2\): the more the box shakes, the more people jump.

Now count what crosses, in an instant \(dt\), the wall between box \(j\) and box \(j+1\). Box \(j\) holds a fraction \(m_j h\) of the population, and half of its \(q_j\) jumps to the right. Box \(j+1\) holds \(m_{j+1} h\), and half of its \(q_{j+1}\) jumps to the left. The net flow is the difference:

\[ J\,dt \;=\; \underbrace{\tfrac12\,\frac{\cR{D_j}\,dt}{h^2}\; m_j\,h}_{\text{from } j \text{ to the right}} \;-\; \underbrace{\tfrac12\,\frac{\cR{D_{j+1}}\,dt}{h^2}\; m_{j+1}\,h}_{\text{from } j+1 \text{ to the left}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad J \;=\; -\,\frac12\;\frac{\cR{D_{j+1}}\,m_{j+1} - \cR{D_j}\,m_j}{h} . \]

The last ratio is a "change in \(D\,m\) over distance"; with narrow boxes it is a derivative:

The flow of luck

\[ J_{\text{luck}} \;=\; -\,\tfrac12\,\partial_x\big(\cR{D}\,\cM{m}\big) \]
  • The minus sign: the flow runs downhill, from where \(D\,m\) is large to where it is small.
  • Why \(D\cdot m\) and not just \(m\): what crosses from each side depends on how many people there are (\(m\)) and how much they shake (\(D\)). If one side is full but nobody there moves (\(D = 0\)), nobody crosses from that side. Since \(\pi^*\) depends on \(x\), \(D\) changes from one box to the next and cannot be taken out of the derivative.
  • The \(\tfrac12\): it is the half that jumps to each side. It is the same \(\tfrac12\) as in Taylor.

The same count, done box by box instead of wall by wall, gives what box \(i\) gains: what reaches it from its two neighbours minus what leaves it. It is a second difference, the discrete version of \(\partial_{xx}\), and it coincides with \(-\partial_x J_{\text{luck}}\):

\[ \Delta m_i \;=\; \frac{dt}{2h^2}\Big(\cR{D_{i-1}}\,m_{i-1} \;-\; 2\,\cR{D_i}\,m_i \;+\; \cR{D_{i+1}}\,m_{i+1}\Big) \;\xrightarrow{\;h\to 0\;}\; \cR{\tfrac12\,\partial_{xx}\big(D\,m\big)}\,dt . \]

The effect is to flatten. At a peak the second derivative is negative and the peak goes down; in a valley it is positive and the valley fills up.

What happens in this problem

With Merton, \(\pi^*\) does not depend on \(x\), so \(D\) is the same everywhere and \(J_{\text{luck}} = -\tfrac12 D\,\partial_x m\). To the left of the peak the density rises, so the flow goes to the left; to the right it falls, so it goes to the right. The population widens on both sides. It is the orange curve in the bookkeeping chart.

With the penalty, \(\pi^*\) drops just below the mean and \(D\) is small there. Those people shake little and leave their area little, while people keep arriving from the sides, so we can expect the population to pile up somewhat just below the mean. This is reasoned, not computed.

7.5 Putting the two parts together: equation (5)

The total flow is the sum of the two:

\[ J \;=\; \underbrace{\cB{b}\,\cM{m}}_{\text{the predictable part}} \;\underbrace{-\;\tfrac12\,\partial_x\big(\cR{D}\,\cM{m}\big)}_{\text{luck}} . \]

Put it into the bookkeeping \(\partial_t m = -\partial_x J\). Differentiating the luck part produces a second derivative, and the two minus signs turn into a plus:

\[ \cT{\partial_t m} \;=\; -\,\partial_x\big(\cB{b}\,m\big) \;+\; \tfrac12\,\partial_{xx}\big(\cR{D}\,m\big) . \]

Move everything to the left and write \(b\) and \(D\) with the equilibrium strategy. Out comes exactly the equation of the problem statement:

Fokker–Planck, equation (5) of the problem statement

\[ \cT{\partial_t m} \;+\; \partial_x\Big[\cB{\big(rx+\cP{\pi^*}(\mu-r)\big)}\,m\Big] \;-\; \tfrac12\,\partial_{xx}\Big[\cR{\sigma^2(\cP{\pi^*})^2}\,m\Big] \;=\; 0, \qquad \cM{m}(0,\cdot)=\mathcal N(1,\,0.2^2) \tag{5} \]

For the curious: it is the same calculation as the HJB, seen from the other side

The average of any function \(f\) over the population is \(\int f\,m\,dx\). Each investor obeys the core calculation, so, averaging over all of them:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\int f\,m\,dx \;=\; \int \Big(\cB{b\,f'} + \cR{\tfrac12 D\,f''}\Big)\,m\,dx . \]

Integrating by parts moves the derivatives from \(f\) to \(m\); each derivative that changes side changes the sign:

\[ \int f\,\cT{\partial_t m}\,dx \;=\; \int f\,\Big(-\partial_x\big(\cB{b}\,m\big) + \tfrac12\,\partial_{xx}\big(\cR{D}\,m\big)\Big)\,dx . \]

Since it holds for any \(f\), what is inside has to match, and that is the Fokker–Planck. The HJB applies the operation \(b\,\partial_x + \tfrac12 D\,\partial_{xx}\) to \(V\); the Fokker–Planck applies its "transpose" to \(m\). It is the same information looked at from two sides.

7.6 What follows: the mean and the width

With equation (5) we can draw conclusions about the population without solving it entirely. The mean. Multiply the equation by \(x\) and integrate over the whole axis. Integrating by parts, and since \(m\) is 0 far away, \(-\int x\,\partial_x(b\,m)\,dx = \int b\,m\,dx\) and \(\tfrac12\int x\,\partial_{xx}(D\,m)\,dx = 0\). It is the same as using \(f(x) = x\) in the box above: the second derivative of \(x\) is zero and the luck term disappears:

\[ \frac{d\cM{\bar m_t}}{dt} \;=\; \int \cB{b}\,m\,dx \;=\; r\,\cM{\bar m_t} \;+\; (\mu-r)\int \cP{\pi^*}(t,x)\,m(t,x)\,dx . \]

Luck pushes equally to both sides: it widens the crowd but does not move its centre. The mean only moves with the predictable part. Solving:

\[ \cM{\bar m_T} \;=\; e^{rT}\,\bar m_0 \;+\; (\mu-r)\int_0^T e^{r(T-t)}\Big(\int \cP{\pi^*}\,m\,dx\Big)\,dt . \]

In words: the final mean is the initial money with interest, plus the risk premium on what the population has invested on average (also with interest). With \(\lambda = 0\) everyone invests \(\pi^*(t) = 1.5\,e^{-r(T-t)}\); with interest, that is worth 1.5 at every moment, and:

\[ \bar m_T \;=\; e^{0.02}\cdot 1 \;+\; 0.06\times 1.5 \;=\; 1.0202 + 0.09 \;\approx\; 1.110 . \]

This is the wire through which the mean learns what the investors do: if they invest less, \(\cM{\bar m_T}\) falls.

The width. If you multiply by \(x^2\) instead of \(x\), luck does show up, because the second derivative of \(x^2\) is 2:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\int x^2\,m\,dx \;=\; \int \big(2x\,\cB{b} + \cR{D}\big)\,m\,dx \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Var}(X_t) \;=\; 2r\,\operatorname{Var}(X_t) + \cR{D} \quad (\lambda = 0). \]

The variance grows for two reasons: interest stretches the population (the \(2r\)) and luck adds \(D\) per unit of time. With \(\lambda = 0\), \(D = \sigma^2\pi^*(t)^2\), and solving:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(X_t) \;=\; 0.04\,e^{2rt} \;+\; 0.09\,t\,e^{-2r(T-t)} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \operatorname{Var}(X_T) = 0.0416 + 0.09 = 0.1316, \quad \text{standard deviation } 0.363 . \]

These are exactly the numbers of the next chart.

8How they are coupled: the equilibrium

The HJB needs one number from the crowd: \(\cM{\bar m_T}\), which is in its final condition. The Fokker–Planck needs the strategy \(\cP{\pi^*(t,x)}\) at every moment, which comes from the HJB. And they run in opposite directions: the HJB starts at \(T\) and the Fokker–Planck at 0, so the two cannot be advanced together, step by step.

Call \(\Phi(\bar m)\) the final mean that comes out when everyone acts believing the mean will be \(\bar m\). The equilibrium is a number that reproduces itself:

\[ \Phi(\cM{\bar m}) \;=\; \cM{\bar m} \qquad \text{(condition (6))} \]

Without this condition, people would act on a mean that then does not come true.

Why there was no game with the original utility

With the utility (O3) of the original statement, \(U = -e^{-\gamma(x-\theta\bar m)}\), where \(\theta\) measures how much the mean matters (it has nothing to do with the weights \(\theta\) of the networks in section 9), the mean comes out as a factor:

\[ -e^{-\gamma(x-\theta\bar m)} \;=\; \underbrace{e^{\gamma\theta\bar m}}_{\text{constant}\,>\,0}\cdot\big(-e^{-\gamma x}\big) . \]

The crowd only multiplies \(V\) by a positive constant, and \(\pi^*\) only looks at the ratio \(\partial_x V/\partial_{xx}V\), where that constant cancels. That is why \(\pi^*\) is Merton for any \(\bar m\), \(\Phi\) is constant and the loop closes in one round. The penalty (2) is added instead of multiplying, and it changes the shape of \(U\) right around \(\bar m\).

How the crowd changes the strategy

At the last instant \(V = U\), so \(\pi^*\) can be computed exactly with formula (4). Above the mean the penalty does not exist and \(\pi^* = 1.5\). Below it:

\[ \cP{\pi^*(T,x)} \;=\; \frac{\mu-r}{\sigma^2}\cdot\frac{\gamma e^{-\gamma x} + \lambda\,(\cM{\bar m_T}-x)}{\gamma^2 e^{-\gamma x} + \lambda} \qquad \big(x < \cM{\bar m_T}\big) . \]
  • Just below the mean, \(\pi^*\) drops: with \(\lambda = 2\) and \(\bar m = 1.1\), from 1.5 to 0.21. There a small loss widens a gap that is paid squared, and the curvature of \(U\) shoots up (\(U'' = -\gamma^2 e^{-\gamma x} - \lambda\)). It is exactly where almost half of the population is.
  • Far below, \(\pi^*\) rises again and, more than \(1/\gamma\) below the mean, exceeds Merton. The value of an extra dollar grows with the gap, and with it the tolerance to risk. There are very few people there (≈ 0.3 % with \(\lambda = 0\)). This qualifies section 13 of the explanatory PDF, which only mentions "playing it safe".
  • When \(\bar m\) moves, the dip moves with it. This is the wire back: the crowd decides where the line is, and the line changes what each person does.
  • This is only at \(t = T\). Before the end \(V\) is smoother, and we can expect a wider and shallower dip, which starts to show a little above the mean. How much is what the project has to compute.

The loop, number by number

A possible shortcut. Since \(V\) only depends on the crowd through one number, a single network \(V(t,x,\bar m)\) could be trained for many \(\bar m\) at once. The equilibrium is then an equation in a single unknown: find the \(\bar m\) with \(\Phi(\bar m) = \bar m\).

9The PINNs with λ > 0: which programs we write and why this way

This section explains how the problem with \(\lambda > 0\) is solved with PINNs: which programs there are, what each one does and why it is done this way. It is rebuilt from scratch from the problem statement and sections 3 to 8. First, what a PINN is; then the recipe at a glance; then each step on its own. The numbers that appear can be redone by hand; what only the programs will know is presented as what is expected. Labels like T2 refer to the tasks of section 10.

Why with λ > 0 we have to compute

With \(\lambda = 0\) everyone invests the same, whatever their wealth, and there is a formula (section 6). With \(\lambda > 0\) not any more: at \(t = T\), just above the mean one invests 1.5 and just below 0.21 (section 8). Merton-type formulas always give the same investment for every wealth, so they do not work, and no other is known: the solution has to be computed. This section does it with networks. Section 10 explains the other two ways of computing it, which serve to check the networks.

What a PINN is, in five ideas

  1. The unknown becomes a network. Instead of looking for the function \(v(t,x)\), we look for the weights \(\theta\) of a network that represents it.
  2. The equation gives a number at each point: the residual. The network is put into the equation. If the network were the solution, 0 would come out; what comes out, \(R(t,x)\), measures how much it fails there.
  3. The derivatives of the network are exact. The network is a chain of simple operations and PyTorch applies the chain rule on its own (automatic differentiation). That is why the neurons use \(\tanh\): with ReLU the second derivative would be 0, and \(\pi^*\), which divides by it, would come out infinite.
  4. The loss is the mean of \(R^2\) at random points of the domain (the collocation points), drawn again at every iteration so that the network does not satisfy the equation only at a few fixed points.
  5. The conditions choose the solution. The equation alone has many solutions: in the HJB of step 1, if \(v\) is a solution, so is \(v + 5\). That is why the loss also adds how much the final condition and the edges fail, each term multiplied by a coefficient (not a "weight", so as not to confuse it with \(\theta\)). Training, with Adam and at the end L-BFGS, is lowering that sum.

A PINN does not learn from data: it learns from the equation and its conditions.

The recipe at a glance

The dish: for each \(\lambda\), three things consistent with each other: the strategy \(\cP{\pi^*(t,x)}\), the spread of wealth \(\cM{m(t,x)}\) and the final mean \(\cM{\bar m_T}\). Consistent means that everyone acts assuming a final mean and that is the mean that comes out.

What decides how the work is split is what each equation needs from the other. It is enough to look at them:

  • The HJB needs a single number from the crowd, \(\cM{\bar m}\), and only in its final condition \(V(T,x) = U(x,\bar m)\).
  • The Fokker–Planck needs a single function from the HJB, the strategy \(\cP{\pi^*(t,x)}\), which goes inside \(b\) and \(D\).

So the programs only pass two things to each other: a number and a function. That gives four programs, A, B, C and D, plus those that check them:

Step 1Program A, the \(V\) network T2

What for: given an assumed final mean \(\bar m\), know how much each investor would invest at each moment, \(\pi^*(t,x)\). In: \(\bar m\). Out: the \(V\) network (weights \(\theta_V\), output \(v\)) and, from it, \(\pi^*\).

\(\bar m\) is treated as a fixed number because a single investor, among infinitely many, does not move the mean. Making the assumed mean the one that really comes out is fixed in step 4.

Why the network gives \(v = \log(-V)\)

We write \(V = -e^{v}\) and the network gives \(v\). Three reasons:

  • \(V\) is always negative, because the utility is. Written this way, the network cannot make it positive.
  • \(V\) changes size enormously and \(v\) does not. In the domain, \(V\) goes from −7 to −0.05. If the network gave \(V\), the errors at the bottom would weigh thousands of times more than those at the top, and the network would neglect the top. \(v\), on the other hand, is almost a straight line. And it is easy to read: \(-v\) is the certainty equivalent, the sure money your situation is worth (with \(\gamma = 1\)). Below the mean it is worth somewhat less than your wealth: that is what the penalty takes away from you.
  • \(\pi^*\) comes out stable, because in its formula the factors \(e^{v}\) cancel and numbers of size 1 are left.

The formulas that go into the code

With \(V = -e^{v}\): \(\partial_t V = -e^{v}v_t\), \(\partial_x V = -e^{v}v_x\) and \(\partial_{xx}V = -e^{v}(v_{xx}+v_x^2)\). Substitute them into the HJB of section 6 and divide everything by \(-e^{v}\), which is never zero:

\[ \begin{aligned} R_{\text{HJB}} &= \cT{v_t} + \cB{r x\,v_x} - \frac{\kappa^2\,v_x^2}{2\,(v_{xx}+v_x^2)} && \text{the residual: must be 0}\\[6pt] \cP{\pi^*} &= -\frac{\mu-r}{\sigma^2}\cdot\frac{v_x}{v_{xx}+v_x^2} && \text{the strategy}\\[6pt] v_T(x) &= \log\Big(e^{-\gamma x} + \tfrac{\lambda}{2}\big((\cM{\bar m}-x)^+\big)^2\Big) && \text{the target at } t = T \end{aligned} \]

With \(\kappa = (\mu-r)/\sigma = 0.3\). \(V\) being concave is the same as the denominator \(v_{xx}+v_x^2\) being positive.

def hjb_residual(value_net, t, x):
    v = value_net(t, x)
    v_t, v_x = d_dt(v, t), d_dx(v, x)            # exact derivatives, with autograd
    v_xx = d_dx(v_x, x)
    den = torch.clamp(v_xx + v_x**2, min=0.05)    # avoids dividing by almost zero at the start
    return v_t + r * x * v_x - kappa**2 * v_x**2 / (2 * den)

The hard part: the step at the mean

At \(t = T\), \(\pi^*\) jumps right at the mean: below it one invests 0.21 and above it 1.5 (the figure above, on the right). The reason is that below the mean the penalty bends the utility a lot, and above it the penalty does not exist. Before \(T\), luck blurs the jump and turns it into a smooth descent, but close to \(T\) it is still almost a step.

A \(\tanh\) network is smooth and finds it hard to draw steps. There are two remedies: putting more points near \((T,\bar m)\) and, if it is still hard, rounding the corner of \((\cdot)^+\) in the target a little (the \(\varepsilon\) of the figure). To know whether the rounding changes the result, repeat with half the \(\varepsilon\): if the final mean does not move, it is fine.

The edges: what the network is told on each side

Each network lives on a rectangle: time from 0 to 1 horizontally and wealth from −1 to 3 vertically. For its equation to have a single solution, it has to be told its value on some sides of the rectangle. The rest it finds by satisfying the equation.

  • Top, \(x = 3\): Merton. Whoever has 3 is so far above the mean that they will not fall below it: for them the penalty does not exist, and their value is Merton's, which has a formula.
  • Bottom, \(x = -1\): an approximation. There the penalty weighs a lot and there is no formula. We use the value that investor would have if they kept playing Merton: Merton's value minus the penalty they would pay on average. It is not exact, but it is close, and its error stays near the edge, far from where people are.
  • Why not leave \(x = -1\) empty. A badly set edge spreads upwards. If the edge "bounced" (nobody can go below −1), the investor at the bottom would bet heavily, because they cannot lose any more; by betting they would move more and drag those above them along.
  • How it is checked: move the bottom edge to −2 and see whether anything changes where people live. If nothing changes, the edge was fine.

As a formula, for the code: \(V(t,-1) \approx V_{\text{Merton}}(t,-1) - \tfrac\lambda2\big[(\bar m - \text{mean})^2 + \text{variance}\big]\), where the mean \(-e^{r(T-t)} + \kappa^2(T-t)/\gamma\) and the variance \((\kappa/\gamma)^2(T-t)\) are those of someone who starts at −1 playing Merton (the calculations of section 7.6, starting at \(x = -1\)).

A trap: a small loss does not mean a good \(\pi^*\)

\(\pi^*\) depends on the curvature of \(v\), and the curvature enters the HJB multiplied by a small number, \(\kappa^2/2 = 0.045\): the little the market pays for taking risk. If the curvature is a little wrong, \(\pi^*\) changes by 1.5 times that little and the residual by only 0.045 times. So an error in \(\pi^*\) shows up in the residual about 33 times smaller: the network can satisfy the equation almost perfectly and have a rather wrong \(\pi^*\).

  • Do not trust the loss: \(\pi^*\) is checked with the tests of step 5.
  • Two helps: give the equation a larger coefficient than the conditions, and impose at \(t = T\), besides the value, its first two derivatives, which are known exactly except right at the mean.

Step 2Program B, the \(\pi^*\) network T4

What for: hand the strategy to the Fokker–Planck in a form that can be differentiated cheaply and without noise. In: the \(V\) network, frozen. Out: a small network (weights \(\theta_\pi\), output \(p \approx \pi^*\)). It is not a PINN: it is an ordinary fit, without an equation.

Why it is needed. The Fokker–Planck contains \(\partial_{xx}(D\,m)\), with \(D = \sigma^2(\pi^*)^2\), so it needs the second derivative of \(\pi^*\). But \(\pi^*\) already carries the second derivative of \(v\): differentiating it twice more means fourth derivatives of the \(V\) network. They are expensive and, above all, unreliable: training only watches up to the second derivative, and an invisible wiggle in \(v\) grows with each derivative. By copying \(\pi^*\) into a small, smooth network, it is enough to differentiate the copy.

Extra advantage: the \(m\) network then accepts any strategy (Merton's or this one), so it can be tested on its own.

How it is copied: by least squares, \(L_\pi = \text{mean}\big((p - \pi^*)^2\big)\), with \(\pi^*\) computed with the \(V\) network at random points. To start with, 3 layers of 32 neurons and about 3 000 Adam iterations.

What accuracy is needed. Each dollar more or less in the stock during the year moves the final mean by 0.06, the premium. The penalty changes the investment of part of the population, so it moves the final mean by a few hundredths; from one \(\lambda\) to another, probably by a few thousandths. To see them, the strategy has to be right to a few hundredths where people live (\(0.06\times0.02 \approx 0.001\)). This holds for the \(V\) network too.

If it goes wrong: the copy smoothing the step near \((T,\bar m)\) is normal and matters little, because it is only abrupt in the last few days. If the copy comes out noisy, the problem is in the \(V\) network, not here.

Step 3Program C, the \(m\) network T4

What for: with everyone playing the strategy \(p\), see how wealth is spread and compute the final mean that comes out, \(\Phi(\bar m)\). In: the \(\pi^*\) network, frozen, and the initial spread \(m_0 = \mathcal N(1,\,0.2^2)\). Out: the \(m\) network (weights \(\theta_m\), output \(q\), with \(m = e^q\)), \(\Phi(\bar m)\) and the final spread.

What we expect it to draw. Just below the mean people hardly invest, so they hardly move, and those who fall from above stay there. We expect people piled up just below the mean and a sharp cut when crossing it, like a traffic jam: where people move less, more of them accumulate (section 7.4). The network needs extra points near \((T,\bar m)\) to draw that cut.

Why \(q = \log m\). A density cannot be negative, and with \(m = e^q\) it never is. Besides, \(m_0\) goes from 2 in the middle to almost 0 at the edges, and its logarithm is a simple parabola, much easier to learn.

Why the residual is measured in \(m\), not in \(q\). That way each point weighs according to how many people are there, which is what matters for the mean. Measured in \(q\), it would demand accuracy in the tails, where there is nobody.

The formulas that go into the code

The residual is the bookkeeping of section 7 with the copied strategy: \(b = rx + (\mu-r)\,p\), \(D = \sigma^2p^2\), the flow \(J = b\,m - \tfrac12\partial_x(D\,m)\) and \(R_{\text{FP}} = \partial_t m + \partial_x J\), which must be 0.

def fp_residual(density_net, strategy_net, t, x):
    m = torch.exp(density_net(t, x))          # m = e^q, always positive
    p = strategy_net(t, x)                    # the copied strategy (step 2)
    b = r * x + (mu - r) * p                  # the predictable part
    D = sigma**2 * p**2                       # luck
    J = b * m - 0.5 * d_dx(D * m, x)          # the flow
    return d_dt(m, t) + d_dx(J, x)            # in minus out: must be 0

The mass. A network does not satisfy the bookkeeping exactly: if the residual has a small bias, the total number of people drifts away from 1. And 2 % too many people is 2 % too much mean, about 0.02, more than the differences we are looking for. There are two remedies: a term in the loss that pushes the mass to 1, and computing the mean by dividing by the mass, \(\Phi(\bar m) = \int x\,m(T,x)\,dx \,\big/ \int m(T,x)\,dx\).

Edges: \(m = 0\) at \(x = -1\) and at \(x = 3\), because nobody gets there (the drawing of step 1).

Step 4Program D, the loop T5

What for: making the mean assumed in step 1 the one that comes out in step 3, condition (6). It is a small program: it calls A, B and C with a \(\bar m\), reads the mean that comes out, \(\Phi(\bar m)\), and if it does not match it starts again with it.

  • The mean that comes out is taken as it is, without mixing it with the previous one. We expect \(\Phi\) to change little when \(\bar m\) changes, because the mean only learns about the strategy through the premium, and so the loop converges in a few rounds. Mixing, as in the figure of section 8, is only needed if the values jump from one side to the other more and more.
  • Each round starts with the networks of the previous one, which hardly have to change, so the following rounds are short.
  • It stops when the difference between the assumed mean and the one that comes out is smaller than the error of the networks themselves.
  • The "all at once" version (the second part of T5) trains the three networks together. Each network must learn only from its own equation (in PyTorch, .detach()), so that none of them bends to make another's job easier.

How the Monte Carlo check works

Monte Carlo means simulating: we create many fictitious investors and make them live through the year step by step with equation (1), each with their own luck. It uses neither the HJB nor the Fokker–Planck, only the wealth equation, and that is why it is a check independent of the networks. It is the same as the figure "10 000 investors against the Fokker–Planck" of section 7.6 does with the Merton strategy; here it is done with the network's strategy.

  1. Draw the initial wealths: from \(m_0 = \mathcal N(1,\,0.2^2)\), or all equal to the same \(x_0\), depending on the test.
  2. Move time forward in small steps \(\Delta t\), for example of 0.001 years. At each step, for everyone at once: look up how much each one invests according to the strategy, \(\pi = p(t, X)\); draw their luck, a normal number \(Z\); and update their wealth, \(X \leftarrow X + \big(rX + (\mu-r)\pi\big)\Delta t + \sigma\pi\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z\).
  3. At the end compute the mean, the standard deviation and the histogram of the wealths, or the average utility.
  4. Compare with the network, knowing how much the simulation can be off by chance: the mean of \(N\) investors is typically off by (standard deviation)\(/\sqrt N\). With the \(10^4\) investors of the proposal that would be about 0.004, of the order of the differences we are looking for; with \(10^5\), about 0.001.
X = sample_m0(N)                                  # initial wealths
for k in range(n_steps):
    t = k * dt
    pi = strategy(t, X)                           # how much each one invests
    Z = rng.standard_normal(N)                    # their luck in this step
    X = X + (r * X + (mu - r) * pi) * dt + sigma * pi * np.sqrt(dt) * Z
mean_T, sd_T = X.mean(), X.std()

With that simulation two different tests are done:

  • The population, for the \(m\) network. Simulate \(10^5\) investors from \(m_0\) with the same strategy \(p\) the \(m\) network used. Their final mean must match the network's \(\Phi(\bar m)\), and their histogram \(m(T,x)\). Since the strategy is the same on both sides, if they do not match, the fault is in the \(m\) network.
  • The value, for the \(V\) network. Simulate many investors who all start with the same wealth \(x_0\) and follow the network's own \(\pi^*\), with the same \(\bar m\). The average of their final utility, \(U(X_T,\bar m)\), must match \(V(0,x_0) = -e^{v(0,x_0)}\), because \(V\) is exactly that: the average utility obtained by playing well. Repeat with a few \(x_0\), for example 0.6, 1.0 and 1.4. It detects a network that fools itself about what its own strategy is worth, for instance because of a badly set edge. What it does not detect well are small errors in \(\pi^*\): near the best strategy the value hardly changes (the top of the parabola in section 6), so a somewhat worse strategy gives almost the same average utility.

The extra task: what finite differences are and whether they are reliable here

The idea. Instead of looking for \(V\) at every point, we look for it only on a grid, for example every 0.01 dollars of wealth and every 0.001 years. Each derivative is replaced by a difference between neighbours on the grid; for example, \(\partial_x V \approx \big(V(x+h) - V(x-h)\big)/(2h)\), with \(h\) the grid step. The equation then becomes a rule to go from one instant to the next: the HJB is run backwards from \(T\) and the Fokker–Planck forwards from 0. The guide already has both ideas: the figure "One step before the end" (section 5) is one step of the HJB backwards, and the bookkeeping of boxes (section 7.2) is the Fokker–Planck on a grid.

Is it viable? Yes. With a single wealth variable, the grid has a few hundred points in \(x\) and a few thousand instants, and each step is a small calculation: a laptop does it in seconds. It is the classic method for equations like these in one dimension, and it is usually more accurate and faster than a PINN. The point of the PINN is something else: it needs no grid, so it can be taken to many dimensions (several assets), where a grid is impossible. The project tests it in the case where everything can be checked.

Is it reliable? Yes, if four known things are taken care of:

  • The HJB is not linear, because \(\partial_{xx}V\) is in the denominator of \(\pi^*\). The usual approach is, at each step backwards, to compute \(\pi^*\) with the \(V\) at hand, solve the step with that \(\pi^*\) fixed (it is then a linear equation, a small system) and repeat two or three times. Solving each step this way, "implicitly", also stops the calculation from blowing up if the time step is large.
  • The step at the mean near \(T\): a fine grid around \(\bar m\).
  • The Fokker–Planck has to conserve people: with the bookkeeping of boxes, what leaves one box enters the neighbour, and the mass stays at exactly 1.
  • The edges: the same as for the PINN (the drawing in step 1 of section 9).

How trust is earned: with \(\lambda = 0\) it has to give Merton and the exact normal, and making the grid finer must not change the result. If both hold, it is a reliable reference for \(\lambda > 0\).

11Cheat sheet

  1. Both equations are the core calculation: time, plus drift times slope, plus \(\tfrac12\) times variance times curvature.
  2. The HJB is that calculation applied to \(V\), plus "choose the best", plus "with the best plan, the value is not expected to change" (the = 0). It runs backwards.
  3. \(\pi^*\) is the top of a parabola: what the market pays per unit of variance, times your risk tolerance.
  4. The Fokker–Planck is bookkeeping plus two flows: \(\partial_t m = -\partial_x J\), with \(J = b\,m - \tfrac12\partial_x(D\,m)\). The predictable part shifts the population and luck widens it. It runs forwards.
  5. They are coupled by two wires: \(\pi^*\) (from the HJB to the Fokker–Planck) and \(\bar m_T\) (back). The equilibrium is the fixed point \(\Phi(\bar m) = \bar m\).
  6. With PINNs there are four programs, which only pass a number and a function to each other: the \(V\) network solves the HJB for an assumed mean (it outputs \(v\), with \(V = -e^v\); \(-v\) is the certainty equivalent), the \(\pi^*\) network copies the strategy so it can be differentiated cheaply (it outputs \(p\)), the \(m\) network solves the Fokker–Planck with that strategy (it outputs \(q\), with \(m = e^q\)) and the loop repeats until \(\Phi(\bar m) = \bar m\). Their weights are called \(\theta\). With \(\lambda > 0\) we expect: a dip in \(\pi^*\) below the mean that sharpens towards \(T\); people piled up just below the mean, with the density dropping suddenly when crossing it; and a \(\Phi\) that changes little with \(\bar m\), with which the loop converges in a few rounds without mixing. And a small loss does not guarantee a good \(\pi^*\): the error in \(\pi^*\) is about 33 times that of the HJB residual.
