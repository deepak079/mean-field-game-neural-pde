How the PINN learns

Method

Method · training the PINN

The network learns its conditions first, the equation last

Hover the chart for every part at one step.

What it shows

The parts of the loss during the first training of the equilibrium search (m = 1.110, from scratch), on a log scale: the four parts of the value network, or the two of the density network. White: 10,000 Adam steps. Shaded: 500 L-BFGS steps. The panel gives each part's start and end value and its share of the final loss.

What to say

  • The network is trained by making each part small at random points: the equation inside, the final value, the final slopes and the edges.
  • The conditions come first: the final value and the edges fall below 10⁻⁴ within 2,000 steps and stay there.
  • The final slopes are the hardest part, about 90% of the loss until step 2,500. They fix the curvature of v at T, which sets π*. L-BFGS divides them by 20.
  • The density network's loss is almost all equation; the mass part stays far below it.

The equilibrium

Results

Results · the equilibrium · λ = 2

The equilibrium is the average that reproduces itself

Start with Back to step 0, then Next once per evaluation.

What it shows

Across: the average final wealth m the investors assume. Up: the average Φ(m) that results when they all play their best strategy against it. The equilibrium is where the dashed FD curve crosses the diagonal. Blue dots: the PINN's three evaluations, joined by the path of the search. Grey horizontal line: the answer without the penalty.

What to say

  • Everyone reacts to one number, the final average, so the equilibrium is a fixed point of one function: Φ(m) = m.
  • Start from the no-penalty answer, 1.110. With the penalty everyone takes less risk, and the average comes out at 1.062.
  • The secant method closes the gap in three PINN evaluations: 5·10⁻², 3·10⁻³, 2·10⁻⁵.
  • Zoom: the PINN lands at 1.06497, FD at 1.06513. The PINN's Φ is 2·10⁻⁴ below FD's at every point, and since Φ is almost flat, that offset passes straight into the answer.

Live

Back and Next step through the search, or click a step chip. Zoom on the crossing animates to the last 0.008 around the answer. Hover a dot for its numbers.

What investors do

Results

Results · the strategy π*(t, x) · λ = 2

Just below the average, investors play safe

Press Play to run time from 0 to 1; it slows down over the last tenth.

What it shows

The equilibrium strategy π*(t, x): dollars in the stock against wealth x, at the time t on the slider. Grey line: Merton, what everyone holds without the penalty. Shaded: where the middle 98% of investors are at that time. Vertical line: the final average mT.

What to say

  • Far above the average, investors hold Merton: the penalty does not reach them.
  • Just below it they cut back to about a third of Merton, because a loss would push them further below.
  • Far below they take more risk than Merton: they already pay the penalty and the stock is their chance to catch up. The switch is near x = mT − 1/γ ≈ 0.065.
  • Play: as time runs out the dip becomes a cliff right at mT. The PINN follows FD wherever investors live; the two part only near x = −1, where nobody is.

Live

Play runs time from 0. Drag the slider, or use the chips for the notebook's snapshot times. Reading guide shows or hides the three grey notes. Hover for both values at one wealth.

Where the wealth ends up

Results

Results · the wealth distribution m(t, x) · λ = 2

The crowd piles up just below the average

Play from t = 0, then Zoom on the cliff at t = 1.

What it shows

The density of wealth m(t, x) at the slider's time: how many investors have wealth x (the area under each curve is 1). Grey: the bell curve without the penalty. Vertical line: mT. Top right: the highest density and the share of investors below mT at that time.

What to say

  • Without the penalty, wealth spreads out as a bell curve.
  • With it, the population is squeezed against the average: many end just below it, few far away.
  • At the end the crowd stops at a cliff at mT: just below, investors hold little stock and their wealth barely moves; just above, they hold Merton and spread out.
  • The PINN follows FD until about t = 0.9, then rounds the cliff off: its peak is 3.5 instead of 4.3 and its total mass 0.998.

Results · Monte Carlo · 1,000 investors · λ = 2

A thousand investors, one strategy

Hover a line to follow one investor and see how much stock they hold.

What it shows

1,000 simulated investors who all follow the FD equilibrium strategy (Euler steps of 0.001 in time, starting from the initial bell curve). Left: each line is one investor's wealth over time, drawn up to the slider's time. Right: the histogram of their wealth at that time, with the PINN and FD densities on top.

What to say

  • Each line is an investor. They start around 1 and all follow the same rule.
  • Watch the end: the lines just below the average go flat, because those investors hold almost no stock. The ones above keep moving.
  • Their histogram builds the same cliff as the Fokker–Planck density.
  • Check with 100,000 investors: their mean at T matches FD's mT within the sampling error (numbers below).

The price of competing

Results

Results · penalty strength λ · FD equilibria

Competing costs money and crowds people below the average

Drag λ, or press Sweep to run it from 0 to 10.

What it shows

FD equilibria for penalty strengths λ from 0 to 10. Top: three numbers about the final population, against λ; the large dot follows the slider and the blue dot is the PINN. Bottom left: the strategy at the start, π*(0, x). Bottom right: the final density m(T, x). Faint lines: the other λ values.

What to say

  • Competing costs money: the average final wealth falls from 1.110 to 1.054, most of it by λ = 2. Investors hold less stock and earn less of its premium.
  • Wealth becomes more equal: the spread falls from 0.36 to 0.27.
  • Yet more investors end below the average, 70% instead of 50%: the penalty makes them end only just below it, in a taller and thinner crowd.
  • The PINN at λ = 2 agrees on the average; its thinner tails and rounded cliff give a smaller spread and fewer investors below.

Where the PINN misses

Backup

Backup · PINN against FD over the whole domain · λ = 2

The PINN only misses at the cliff and at the empty edge

Hover any cell for the gap and its sign.

What it shows

Time across, wealth up. Each cell is the gap between the PINN's strategy and FD's, |π*PINN − π*FD|, in cents, grouped in four bands. Black curves: where investors live (FD density above 0.05). Grey line: mT.

What to say

  • Inside the investors' zone, up to t = 0.9, the PINN is within 7 cents of FD.
  • Two spots differ: the cliff just above mT in the last tenth, where π* jumps and a smooth network rounds it off, and the edge x = −1, where nobody lives.
  • The value V agrees within half a percent everywhere. A small error in V can still be a large one in π*, which depends on its curvature.

Accuracy for the time spent

Results

Results · accuracy and cost · HJB at m̄ = 1.1

In one dimension, the grid is 10⁴ times cheaper

Hover a dot for its run.

What it shows

Both methods solve only the HJB, for a fixed m = 1.1. Across: computing time; up: the largest error of π* where investors live (0 ≤ x ≤ 2.2, t ≤ 0.9), both on log scales. FD: finer and finer grids. PINN: longer and longer training. Left: no penalty, against the exact answer. Right: λ = 2, against FD on a 6,400 × 3,200 grid.

What to say

  • FD improves steadily: each doubling of the grid divides the error by 2 to 4.
  • The PINN does not: without the penalty its error stays between 0.003 and 0.012 whatever the training length.
  • At equal accuracy FD is about 10⁴ times faster here (the dotted line).
  • That is expected in one dimension. A grid needs nd points for d state variables; a PINN samples random points. That is why the PINN is the method for the game with N investors, where the grid is out of reach.

Summary

Summary · PINN against FD · λ = 2

Same equilibrium, very different cost

A static slide: the numbers are the figure.

What it shows

The two methods side by side at λ = 2. Errors are against FD on a 3,200 × 1,600 grid (four times finer in each direction); the π* error is the largest gap where investors live, up to t = 0.9.

What to say

  • Both methods find the same equilibrium, 1.065 against 1.110 without the penalty, within 1.5·10⁻⁴ of each other.
  • The PINN gets there from the equations alone: no grid and no scheme to design.
  • Its weak spot is the end of the horizon at the average, where π* jumps.
  • FD is far cheaper in one dimension; the PINN is the one that carries over to richer games.

Rebuilding the data

Every number on this page is in figure_data.js, written by Ander's script exportar_datos_graficos.py, which is kept in his private folder, not in the repo. The script loads the networks that the results notebook saved in results/results_notebook/, runs the FD solves again (they take seconds), simulates the investors, and checks nothing by hand. Moving a figure into the slides

  1. Copy the fonts <link> and the CSS between the markers figures: shared slide styles and end of shared slide styles.
  2. Include figure_data.js, then the script block marked figures (helpers and one builder per figure).
  3. On the slide (a .s-light or .s-dark section of the 1920 × 1080 stage), add <div class="fig-root fig" data-fig="strategy"></div>: it sits at 112 px from the left and 250 px from the top, 1,696 × 740 px, under the slide title. Then call Figures.build(element).
  4. Each figure carries its own controls and tooltip, and reads its colours from the slide, so it works on light and dark slides.
