The equilibrium from the inside

How the equilibrium with neural networks has been programmed in practice: what each of the three networks learns, with which points and which loss, how they are chained together, how the final mean is read and how the loop over \(\bar m\) is closed. Finite differences only serve as a check, and they are summarised at the end.

Colours: each one always means the same thing

  • Timewhat changes because the clock runs: \(\partial_t\)
  • The predictable partthe drift of wealth: \(rx + \pi(\mu - r)\)
  • Luckthe noise, \((\sigma\pi)^2\), and the curvature
  • The decisionhow much to invest: \(\pi^*\)
  • The crowdthe density \(m\) and its mean \(\bar m\)

Where the numbers come from

The settings (points, steps, step sizes) are the defaults of the code. The formulas and the calculations in the boxes can be redone by hand. Results that only the code gives carry the mark docstring. The interactives are illustrations: they draw the points with their own random draw, use the exact solution for \(\lambda = 0\) or a toy function, and say so.

1The map

The equilibrium fits in one number: the final mean \(\cM{\bar m}\) that everyone assumes has to be the one that really comes out. With networks, checking one \(\bar m\) costs three trainings in a row. The recipe:

  1. Assume a final mean \(\cM{\bar m}\). With \(\bar m\) fixed, each network solves a problem that no longer depends on the others.§8
  2. Train the V network. It solves the HJB and gives how much each person invests: \(\cP{\pi^*(t,x)}\).§4
  3. Copy \(\pi^*\) into a small network. To have a smooth \(\pi^*\) that can be differentiated twice.§5
  4. Train the density network with that copy. It solves Fokker–Planck: where people are at each instant, \(\cM{m(t,x)}\).§6
  5. Read the final mean, \(\Phi(\bar m)\), with the mean equation. It is the mean that comes out if everyone assumes \(\bar m\).§7
  6. Correct \(\bar m\) with the secant and go back to step 2, starting from the previous networks. Until \(\Phi(\bar m) = \bar m\).§8

The V network is Ander's; the copy network, the density network and the loop are Deepak's. Finite differences do steps 2 to 5 on a grid and serve to check the results of the networks (section 9).

2How a network learns

src/mfg/pinn/nets.py · the training loops of hjb.py and fokker_planck.py

The minimum needed to read the rest. It holds for all three networks.

  1. A network is a formula with weights. \(t\) and \(x\) go in and a number comes out. The weights \(\theta\) are about 12 700 adjustable numbers (4 layers of 64 neurons with tanh), and training is choosing them. The derivatives of the network in \(t\) and \(x\) come out exact, by automatic differentiation.
  2. The loss measures how much it fails, at random points. Inside the rectangle \([0, 1] \times [-1, 3]\), whether the network satisfies its equation; on the known sides, whether it gives the known value. It is zero if it satisfies everything.
  3. Adam lowers it, step by step. Each step draws new points and moves the weights a little in the direction in which the loss goes down. With new points every time, the network has to satisfy the equation everywhere, not only at a few places. After thousands of steps, L-BFGS polishes with a fixed draw.
  4. Without stepping through time. Each step adjusts the whole rectangle at once. The direction of time is only in which side is known: the end for the HJB, the start for Fokker–Planck.
  5. One network at a time. Each network is trained with the others frozen. The HJB and Fokker–Planck only pass \(\bar m\) and \(\pi^*\) to each other; the back and forth between them is the secant loop.§8

Decisions, and why

The same network for all three

A single piece of code (nets.py) with exact and smooth derivatives, tested once.

New points at every step

The network cannot memorise a few particular points: it has to satisfy the equation over the whole domain. The copy network is different, because it learns from data and not from an equation (section 5).

Adam first, L-BFGS at the end

Adam copes well with the start, with random weights and changing points. L-BFGS uses the curvature of the loss and goes down much faster once it is close to the minimum.

Training in turns

Each network learns its own equation with the others fixed. That way each loss is that of a known problem, and no derivative goes through another network. Training all of them jointly at once is left out (README).

4The V network, seen from the equilibrium

src/mfg/pinn/hjb.py (Ander's; its guide is pinn_value_network_guide.html, in this folder)

For the equilibrium it is a box: it receives \(\bar m\) and gives \(\cP{\pi^*(t,x)}\), with formula (4) and the exact derivatives of \(v_{\theta_V}\). In the loop it is trained in full the first time and, afterwards, from a copy of the previous one (section 8). Its limit is inherited by the other two: near \((T, \bar m)\) it rounds off the jump of \(\pi^*\) from 1.5 to 0.214, and it is off by 0.1 at \(t = 0.95\) and by almost 1 at \(T\) docstring.

5The network that copies π*

src/mfg/pinn/fokker_planck.py · fit_position_net

To have a smooth \(\pi^*\) that can be differentiated twice without the noise shooting up.

Why it is needed

The Fokker–Planck equation (5) contains \(\partial_x[(rx + (\mu - r)\pi^*)\,m]\) and \(\tfrac12\partial_{xx}[\sigma^2(\pi^*)^2 m]\). Expanding those derivatives produces \(\partial_x \pi^*\) and \(\partial_{xx}\pi^*\). But \(\pi^*\) already uses the second derivative of \(v\). Differentiating it twice more would require the third and fourth derivatives of the V network.

The problem is that each derivative multiplies a wiggle by its frequency. Every trained network has small wiggles, and near the corner \((T, \bar m)\) they are not so small.

Calculation by hand

A wiggle \(a \sin(kx)\) has an \(n\)-th derivative of size \(a\,k^n\). With \(a = 0.001\) and 10 waves per unit (\(k = 2\pi \cdot 10 \approx 62.8\)):

  • 1st derivative: \(0.063\);
  • 2nd: \(3.9\) (already more than the smooth part);
  • 3rd: \(248\);
  • 4th: \(15\,600\).

How it is done

points ← 200 000 (t, x), drawn only once:
         20 % over the whole domain
         40 % with x between 0 and 2.2                    # where the population lives
         40 % with x between m − 0.3 and m + 0.3          # the valley of π*
target ← π* of the V network at those points       # with NumPy, 20 000 at a time
net ← new, or a copy of the previous copy network

repeat 30 000 times:                              # Adam
    batch ← 4000 of the 200 000 points
    loss ← mean of (net(t, x) − target)²
    one step; the step size falls from 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁵
L-BFGS, up to 500 iterations, on 50 000 of the points

freeze the net                                     # its weights no longer change
rms ← typical error over the 200 000 points
\[ L_\pi(\theta_\pi) = \frac{1}{4000}\sum_{i=1}^{4000}{\Big(p_{\theta_\pi}(t_i, x_i) - \pi^*(t_i, x_i)\Big)^2} \]

It is a regression: there is no equation, only values to copy. A tanh network fitted by least squares to many points does not follow fine wiggles that change from one point to another; it averages them. Its output is smooth, and so are its derivatives. The density network uses this copy and its exact derivatives.

Decisions, and why

A separate network, fitted by regression

It gives smooth derivatives of \(\pi^*\) without going through the third and fourth derivatives of the V network. What is lost is small: the error of the copy, weighted by the density, is about 0.002 with \(\lambda = 2\) and \(\bar m = 1.1\) docstring.

Points 20 / 40 / 40

Accuracy is put where people live and where \(\pi^*\) changes fastest (the valley around \(\bar m\)). 20 % spread over the whole domain is enough for the copy not to make anything up far away.

Fixed points and batches

Since it learns from data and not from an equation, a fixed set is enough. Asking the V network for the 200 000 values of \(\pi^*\) only once is cheaper than asking at every step.

Frozen afterwards

While the density is trained, the copy does not change: the density learns with a fixed \(\pi^*\), as Fokker–Planck requires.

For the final mean, the π* of the V network

In the mean equation (section 7) \(\pi^*\) enters without derivatives, and there the V network's is the most faithful. The copy is only used where derivatives are needed.

6The density network

src/mfg/pinn/fokker_planck.py · DensityNet, fp_residual, density_net_loss, train_density_net · tests/test_pinn_fokker_planck.py

With the strategy \(\cP{\pi^*}\) already fixed, learn where the population is at each instant: the density \(\cM{m(t,x)}\) that satisfies Fokker–Planck (5) and starts at \(m_0 = N(1, 0.2^2)\).

It is the last of the three networks of each evaluation of \(\Phi\). It receives the copy of \(\pi^*\) (section 5), frozen, and hands over the density, with which the final mean is computed (section 7). It works in five steps:

  1. The formula. The network gives a number, \(\psi_{\theta_m}(t,x)\), and the density is built from it: \(m = m_0\,e^{t\psi}\).
  2. The equation, at 6000 points. At each step, 6000 new points: 4000 over the whole rectangle and 2000 where the population lives. At each one, does the network satisfy Fokker–Planck?
  3. The mass, at 16 instants. At each step, 16 new instants. At each one, does the density add up to 1?
  4. Train. The loss adds those two parts. The first time, 10 000 Adam steps and 500 of L-BFGS. Afterwards, inside the loop, it starts from the previous network and trains less.
  5. Read. The density, its mass and its mean at any \((t, x)\), and the final mean \(\Phi(\bar m)\).

1. The formula

\[ \cM{q_{\theta_m}(t,x)} = \underbrace{\log m_0(x)}_{\text{the start, fixed}} + \underbrace{\cT{t}}_{\text{the instant}} \cdot \underbrace{\psi_{\theta_m}(t,x)}_{\text{the network}}, \qquad \cM{m_{\theta_m}(t,x)} = e^{\,q_{\theta_m}(t,x)} \]
  • \(\psi_{\theta_m}\): the network. It is the same TanhNet as the other two: \(t\) and \(x\) go in, a number comes out.
  • \(\theta_m\): its weights, about 12 700. It is the only thing that is trained.
  • \(\log m_0(x) = -(x-1)^2/0.08 + 0.69\): the logarithm of the initial density, \(N(1, 0.2^2)\). It is a fixed parabola, with no weights.
  • \(q_{\theta_m}\): what comes out of the formula, the logarithm of the density.
  • \(m_{\theta_m} = e^{q_{\theta_m}}\): the density, which is what is used afterwards.

What \(q\) is, and what \(\psi\) is. \(q = \log m\) is the logarithm of the density: how many people there are near \(x\) at time \(t\), on a logarithmic scale. If \(q\) rises by 1, the density is multiplied by \(e \approx 2.7\); if it falls by 10, it is divided by 22 000. \(\psi = (q - \log m_0)/t\) is how much \(\log m\) has changed since the start, divided by the time that has passed: the average rate at which it has changed. The starting bell is already provided by \(\log m_0\), so the network only learns the change.

Why \(e^{q}\) is exactly \(m\). It is not something that comes out of a calculation: it is how it is defined. The order is the opposite of what it seems. We do not compute \(m\) first and then its logarithm. The network gives \(\psi_{\theta_m}\); from it \(q_{\theta_m}\) is built, and the network's density is defined as \(m_{\theta_m} = e^{q_{\theta_m}}\). What makes that \(m_{\theta_m}\) resemble the true density is not the formula but the training: the loss forces it to satisfy Fokker–Planck and to add up to 1.

Writing it this way leaves no density out:

  • Every positive number is \(e\) raised to something. \(2 = e^{0.69}\) and \(4\cdot 10^{-22} = e^{-49.3}\). That "something" is its logarithm. So every positive density is \(m = e^{q}\) with \(q = \log m\).
  • Every \(q\) can be written as \(\log m_0 + t\cdot\psi\), for \(t > 0\): just take \(\psi = (q - \log m_0)/t\).

It is a change of name, like giving a distance in miles instead of kilometres. The network looks for \(\psi\) instead of \(m\), and from \(\psi\) we get back to \(m\) without losing anything.

There and back, without the penalty

  • From \(m\) to \(\psi\). In the middle, \(x = 1\), at the end, \(t = 1\), the exact density is 1.05. Its logarithm is \(q = 0.049\). At the start, \(\log m_0(1) = 0.69\). So \(\psi = (0.049 - 0.69)/1 = -0.64\).
  • From \(\psi\) to \(m\). \(q = 0.69 + 1\cdot(-0.64) = 0.049\), and \(e^{0.049} = 1.05\). The same density comes out.

Why some carry \(\theta_m\) and others do not. The subscript marks what depends on the weights, that is, what changes during training. Only \(\psi\) is a network, so only its weights are trained. \(q\) and \(m\) carry \(\theta_m\) because they are built from \(\psi_{\theta_m}\): moving the weights changes \(\psi\), and with it \(q\) and \(m\). \(\log m_0\) and \(t\) do not carry it. There are, therefore, three kinds of functions, as in the V network:

  • The exact ones, without a subscript: \(m\), \(q = \log m\) and \(\psi = (q - \log m_0)/t\). They are the true solution, which is not known, except with \(\lambda = 0\).
  • The network's, with \(\theta_m\): \(\psi_{\theta_m}\), \(q_{\theta_m}\) and \(m_{\theta_m}\). Training is bringing them closer to the exact ones.
  • The known ones: \(m_0\) and \(\log m_0\). They are fixed formulas.

Why a logarithm. There are three reasons:

  • Always positive. \(m = e^q\) is positive whatever the network gives. A network that gave \(m\) directly could give negative values, that is, negative people, and they would have to be punished in the loss.
  • Numbers of normal size. At the start, the density is 2 in the middle and \(4\cdot 10^{-22}\) at the edges. A network makes errors of roughly the same size everywhere: an error of \(10^{-3}\), small in the middle, would wipe out those tiny values or make them negative. In logarithm they go from 0.69 to −49, ordinary numbers.
  • A simple shape. The logarithm of a normal is a parabola. With \(\lambda = 0\) the density is a normal at every instant, so \(q\) is a parabola at every instant.

What would go wrong if the network gave \(m\) directly. Nothing stops us from writing \(m_{\theta_m} = \psi_{\theta_m}\) and training with the same loss. The problem is in the training, in three places:

  • Zero also satisfies the equation. Fokker–Planck has no term without \(m\), so \(m = 0\) everywhere gives zero residual. A network that gave \(m\) would have an easy shortcut: flatten itself towards zero. Only a part of the loss for the start and the mass part would hold it back, and the three parts would each pull their own way. With \(\log m_0 + t\,\psi\) the start is exact and \(m\) is never zero, so that shortcut does not exist.
  • The network's error is the same size everywhere. If the network is off by 0.01, in the middle (\(m \approx 2\)) that is 0.5 %. But at the edge \(x = -1\) at the end, where \(m = 5\cdot 10^{-8}\), an error of 0.01 is 200 000 times the value. That error can go downwards and give negative people. If the network gives \(q = \log m\), an error of 0.01 in \(q\) is 1 % of \(m\) anywhere, in the middle and at the edge.
  • The shape. The network would have to draw a narrow bell, which in the middle rises from almost 0 to 2 within a few tenths of \(x\). In logarithm it is a smooth parabola.

At the top, the edges are zeros you cannot see; in the middle they are ordinary numbers, and the two curves are parabolas. At the bottom, \(\psi\) is of size one in the middle and grows a lot towards the edges, where there is almost nobody.

Why multiply the network by \(t\), instead of leaving it free. There are three ways of writing it, and the start is what sets them apart:

  • \(q = \psi\), the network alone. The network would have to learn the whole starting bell, with values of −49 at the edges, and also make exactly \(\log m_0\) come out at \(t = 0\).
  • \(q = \log m_0 + \psi\), without the factor \(t\). The bell is already given, but at \(t = 0\) the network would have to give \(\psi = 0\) everywhere. Nothing forces it to: a freshly created network gives arbitrary values. A third part would be needed in the loss, "\(\psi(0, x)\) must be 0", and it would be only half met, pulling against the other two.
  • \(q = \log m_0 + t\,\psi\), the one in the code. At \(t = 0\) the term \(t\,\psi\) is zero, whatever the weights. So \(m = m_0\) exactly, without asking the loss for it.

The start matters so much because the equation alone is not enough. Fokker–Planck says how people move, but not where they start: each initial spread gives a different solution, and all of them have zero residual. The only thing that picks the right one is the start. And an error at the start is dragged along to the end. With \(\lambda = 0\), if the starting spread were shifted by 0.01, the final mean \(\bar m_T\) would shift by \(0.01 \cdot e^{rT} \approx 0.0102\).

Seen the other way round, \(\psi = (q - \log m_0)/t\): that is why it is sometimes said that we divide by \(t\). It is the same formula, solved for \(\psi\). The network does not learn how much \(\log m\) has changed, but that change divided by time, its average rate. And everything else is still in the network's hands: as soon as \(t > 0\), \(\psi\) can take whatever value is needed.

Two more things about this form:

  • The edges too. At \(x = -1\) and \(x = 3\) the initial density is \(4\cdot 10^{-22}\). For \(m\) to reach \(10^{-4}\) there, \(t\,\psi\) would have to be 40. People start 10 standard deviations from each edge and do not get there, so nobody escapes and nothing needs to be imposed at the edges. The grid, on the other hand, has to impose that nothing goes through them.
  • Why \(t\) and not \(t^2\). At the start, \(\log m\) moves away from \(\log m_0\) in proportion to \(t\), because the equation gives it a finite rate of change. With the factor \(t\), \(\psi\) at \(t = 0\) is exactly that rate, an ordinary number. With \(t^2\), \(\psi\) would have to be the rate divided by \(t\): infinite at \(t = 0\), and a network cannot give that.

Calculation by hand, without the penalty

  • The rate in the middle. The variance of the population is \(V(t) = 0.04\,e^{2rt} + \kappa^2\,t\,e^{-2r(T-t)}\), with \(\kappa^2 = 0.09\). At the start, \(x = 1\) is the middle of the bell, where the density is its height, \(1/\sqrt{2\pi V}\). So \(\log m = -\tfrac12 \log(2\pi V)\), which falls at the rate \(\tfrac12 V'/V\). Since \(V'(0) = 0.0016 + 0.0865 = 0.088\), the rate is \(\tfrac12 \cdot 0.088/0.04 = 1.10\): \(\psi\) is −1.10 at \(t = 0\).
  • The edge. At \(x = -1\), at the end, \(t\,\psi = 32.5\): the density has been multiplied by \(e^{32.5} \approx 10^{14}\), and even so it is only \(5\cdot 10^{-8}\).

The V network does not give its final condition the same form, and rightly so. \(U\) changes curvature suddenly at \(\bar m\), which is what makes \(\pi^*\) jump at \(T\). Adding it at every instant would make \(\pi^*\) jump over the whole rectangle, when it really only jumps at \(T\). That is why its final condition goes in the loss.

2. The equation: the first part of the loss

\[ L_{\text{equation}}(\theta_m) = \frac{1}{6000}\sum_{i=1}^{6000}{R_{\mathrm{FP}}(t_i, x_i; \theta_m)^2} \] \[ R_{\mathrm{FP}} = \cT{\partial_t m_{\theta_m}} + \partial_x\Big[\cB{\big(rx + (\mu - r)\,p_{\theta_\pi}\big)}\, \cM{m_{\theta_m}}\Big] - \tfrac12\,\partial_{xx}\Big[\cR{\sigma^2 p_{\theta_\pi}^2}\, \cM{m_{\theta_m}}\Big] \]

What it measures. Whether the network satisfies Fokker–Planck (5). \(R_{\mathrm{FP}}\) is equation (5) term by term, with \(m_{\theta_m}\) in place of \(m\) and the copy \(p_{\theta_\pi}\) in place of \(\pi^*\). Its three terms are the change in time, what the drift moves and what luck spreads. It is zero where the network satisfies the equation.

The points. 6000, new at every step, all with \(t\) at random between 0 and 1: 4000 with \(x\) at random between −1 and 3, over the whole rectangle, and 2000 with \(x\) between 0 and 2.2, where the population lives.

Why 2000 more between 0 and 2.2. Almost everyone is there: with \(\lambda = 0\), at the end, the mean is 1.11 and the standard deviation 0.36, and between 0 and 2.2 there is room for three standard deviations on each side. With uniform points only, 55 % would land there (2.2 of the 4 of width); with the 2000 extra, 70 %. The 4000 over the whole rectangle are still needed, so that the network does not make anything up outside.

Why it is measured in \(m\) and not in \(\log m\). That way each point weighs according to how many people are there. Where \(m\) is tiny the residual is tiny too, whatever \(\psi\) is, and the loss asks nothing of the network. That is exactly where \(\psi\) would have to be huge: between 30 and 100 at \(x = -1\) with \(\lambda = 0\). Written for \(q\), dividing by \(m\), all points would weigh the same, and the network would also have to be right where nobody lives.

Why the copy of \(\pi^*\). Taking the derivatives of (5) produces \(\partial_x \pi^*\) and \(\partial_{xx} \pi^*\). With the V network those would be its third and fourth derivatives, with a lot of noise; the copy is smooth (section 5). It is frozen, so while the density is trained the strategy does not change.

How it is computed.

m            ← exp(q(t, x))                    # density network
p            ← copy network(t, x)              # π*, frozen
drift_flow   ← (r·x + (μ − r)·p) · m
luck_flow    ← (σ·p)² · m
R ← ∂t m + ∂x drift_flow − ½ ∂xx luck_flow     # automatic differentiation

There is no need to write the product rule by hand. When differentiating the two "flows", automatic differentiation produces \(\partial_x p\) and \(\partial_{xx} p\) from the copy by itself, and \(\partial_t q\), \(\partial_x q\) and \(\partial_{xx} q\) from the density network.

3. The mass: the second part

\[ L_{\text{mass}}(\theta_m) = \frac{1}{16}\sum_{k=1}^{16}{\Big(\int_{-1}^{3}{m_{\theta_m}(t_k, x)\,dx} - 1\Big)^2} \]

What it measures. Whether the total population is still 1 at each instant: that nobody appears or disappears.

The points. 16 instants \(t_k\) at random between 0 and 1, new at every step. At each one, the integral is done with the trapezoid rule over 401 points of \(x\) between −1 and 3, 0.01 apart. That is \(16 \cdot 401 = 6416\) evaluations of the network, as many as in the equation.

Why it is needed, if the equation already conserves mass. Integrating (5) over \(x\), the drift and luck terms only leave what goes through the edges, which is almost nothing, and what is left is:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\int_{-1}^{3}{m_{\theta_m}\,dx} = \int_{-1}^{3}{R_{\mathrm{FP}}\,dx} \]

With zero residual, the mass does not change. But some residual is always left, and the mass changes at that rate, building up over time. This part holds it at 1.

Why 16 instants and 401 points. They are reasonable starting values, not tuned ones. With a step of 0.01, the trapezoid rule over a bell of width 0.2 or more has a negligible error. And since the 16 instants change at every step, over the training the mass is checked over the whole time interval.

The total loss adds the two parts, each with coefficient 1 (not tuned either):

\[ L_m(\theta_m) = L_{\text{equation}}(\theta_m) + L_{\text{mass}}(\theta_m) \]

4. The training

net ← new, with random weights; or a copy of the network of the previous evaluation
repeat N times:                                     # Adam
    6000 new points: 4000 over the whole rectangle, 2000 with x in [0, 2.2]
    16 new instants, each with 401 points in x
    loss ← mean of R² + mean of (mass − 1)²
    one Adam step, with a step size that keeps falling
fix one draw of points
L-BFGS, up to K iterations, on that draw
  • The first time, from random weights: \(N = 10\,000\) Adam steps, with the step size falling from \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-4}\), and \(K = 500\).
  • Inside the loop, afterwards, from a copy of the network of the previous evaluation: \(N = 2000\), from \(3\cdot 10^{-4}\) to \(10^{-4}\), and \(K = 300\). Between two nearby \(\bar m\) the density hardly changes (section 8).

The copy of \(\pi^*\) stays frozen all the time. The seed of the draw is fixed, so each training always gives the same result.

5. What is read at the end

The trained network gives the density at any \((t, x)\) and, adding over \(x\), its mass and its mean; also the residual, to see where it fails. The final mean \(\Phi(\bar m)\) is not taken from the mean of the density at \(T\), but from the mean equation, which is about ten times more accurate (section 7).

Where it fails: the cliff near T

Near \(T\) people pile up just below \(\bar m\) and the density drops suddenly when crossing it. The reason is luck. Just below, people invest little (\(\pi^* = 0.214\) at \(T\)), so the noise is small and people stay put. Just above, they invest like Merton (1.5): the noise is seven times larger and people spread out.

With the grid, the density goes from 4 to 0.6 within a hundredth. A smooth network cannot produce that step and rounds it off: the density is within 0.09 of the right one for \(t \le 0.75\), within 0.3 at \(t = 0.9\), and drifts up to 1.4 away at the cliff docstring. It hardly affects the final mean, which is read another way (section 7).

How it was checked

  • The residual, against exact answers (tests). With \(\lambda = 0\), the exact normal and Merton's \(\pi^*\) give zero residual (less than \(10^{-10}\)). With a \(\pi\) that changes with \(x\), the residual matches the finite-difference one, so the derivatives of \(\pi\) enter correctly.
  • The form (test). Untrained, the network already gives \(m_0\) at \(t = 0\).
  • A short training with λ = 0 (test: small network, on the CPU, about 25 s). It gives the exact normal: the mean within 0.006, the density within 0.007 and the residual below 0.04. The mass is required within \(2\cdot 10^{-4}\); on this computer it ends between \(3\cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(10^{-3}\) and that check fails. 7The final mean

src/mfg/pinn/fokker_planck.py · DensityNetSolution.mean_by_equation

Read \(\Phi(\bar m)\) from the networks with the smallest possible error.

The direct way, and what goes wrong. The natural thing would be to look at where people are at the end and average: \(\Phi = \int x\,m_{\theta_m}(T,x)\,dx\). With the exact density it would come out right. But the network does not satisfy Fokker–Planck exactly: at each point a residual \(R\) is left, which is people the network creates or loses by mistake at that point and that instant. Those errors add up until \(T\).

It can be seen with a calculation. The network's equation is \(m_t = R - (b\,m)_x + \tfrac12 (s^2 m)_{xx}\). Multiplying by \(x\) and integrating (by parts; there are no people at the edges), the network's mean changes like this:

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\int x\,m\,dx = \underbrace{\int b\,m\,dx}_{\text{what moves people}} + \underbrace{\int x\,R\,dx}_{\text{the network's error}} \]

The direct mean carries the second term inside, accumulated from 0 to \(T\): each person created or lost by mistake moves the mean according to where they are. The code's route keeps only the first. Since \(b = r\,x + (\mu - r)\,\pi^*\), that first term is \(r\,M + (\mu - r)\,\mathbb{E}_m[\pi^*]\), with \(M\) the mean: the equation below.

The code's route. Taking averages in the wealth equation (1), the noise (the term with \(dW\)) has mean zero, and an equation for the mean \(M(t)\) of the population is left:

\[ \frac{dM}{dt} = \cB{r\,M} + (\mu - r)\, \mathbb{E}_{m_t}\big[\cP{\pi^*}\big], \qquad M(0) = 1, \qquad \Phi(\bar m) = M(T) \]

Solved, it says something very clear: the final mean is the interest on the initial capital plus what the population's average position earns over the year.

\[ \Phi(\bar m) = e^{rT} + (\mu - r)\int_0^T{e^{r(T-s)}\, \bar\pi(s)\,ds}, \qquad \bar\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_{m_s}[\pi^*(s,\cdot)] \]

Calculation by hand

  • With λ = 0, \(\pi^* = 1.5\,e^{-r(T-s)}\) does not depend on \(x\), so \(\bar\pi\) equals that with any density. It gives \(\Phi = e^{0.02} + 0.06 \cdot 1.5\) \(= 1.0202 + 0.09 = 1.110\), Merton's mean, even if the density network were wrong. (That is why the equilibrium test with \(\lambda = 0\) does not test the density.)
  • With λ = 2, the equilibrium is at 1.0651 docstring. Then \(\int e^{r(T-s)}\bar\pi\,ds\) \(= (1.0651 - 1.0202)/0.06 \approx 0.75\): over the year, the population holds on average half as much in the stock as without the penalty.

Why it is more accurate:

  • The density only enters as weights for the average of \(\pi^*\). An error in the density changes the result only through how much \(\pi^*\) changes with \(x\), and multiplied by \(\mu - r = 0.06\).
  • \(\pi^*\) enters as it is, without derivatives.

With \(\bar m = 1.1\), the direct mean gives 1.06356 and the mean equation 1.062745, against 1.062810 from the grid docstring: errors of \(7.5\cdot 10^{-4}\) and \(6.5\cdot 10^{-5}\).

t ← 401 instants in [0, 1];   x ← 2001 points in [−1, 3]
m ← density network on that grid                 # a 401 × 2001 table
for each instant t_i:
    weights    ← m(t_i, ·) / ∫ m(t_i, x) dx      # the density, with mass 1
    mean_π[i]  ← ∫ weights · π*(t_i, x) dx       # π* of the V network
M ← 1
for i = 0, …, 399:                              # trapezoid rule in t
    M ← [M·(1 + r·h/2) + h·(μ − r)·(mean_π[i] + mean_π[i+1])/2] / (1 − r·h/2)
Φ(m) ← M

Decisions, and why

Φ by the mean equation

About ten times more accurate than the direct mean, because the density only weighs and \(\pi^*\) is not differentiated.

The π* of the V network, not the copy's

No derivatives of \(\pi^*\) are needed here, and the V network's is the original.

Weights divided by the mass

The network's mass is not exactly 1. Normalising at each instant keeps that error out of the mean.

Trapezoid rule in time

The equation is linear in \(M\), and with 400 steps the integration error is negligible compared with that of the networks.

8The loop over m

src/mfg/pinn/equilibrium.py · src/mfg/equilibrium.py

Find the \(\bar m\) with \(\Phi(\bar m) = \bar m\). Each evaluation of \(\Phi\) is three trainings, so few must be spent and the later ones made short.

previous ← none
evaluate Φ(m):
    settings  ← full if previous = none; short otherwise
    if there is a previous one: start from copies of its three networks
    V network    ← train the HJB with m
    copy network ← fit to the π* of the V network
    m network    ← train Fokker–Planck with the copy network
    Φ            ← M(T), by the mean equation
    previous     ← this evaluation
    return Φ

m ← secant(evaluate Φ, from 1.110, tol = 2·10⁻⁴, at most 8 rounds)

The secant draws the line through the last two points \((\bar m, g)\), with \(g = \Phi - \bar m\), and takes the point where it crosses zero. The first two points are 1.110 (the equilibrium without the penalty) and \(\Phi(1.110)\).

\[ \cM{\bar m_{k+1}} = \cM{\bar m_k} - g(\bar m_k)\,\frac{\bar m_k - \bar m_{k-1}}{g(\bar m_k) - g(\bar m_{k-1})} \]

Warm start

The first evaluation trains the three networks from scratch. The following ones start from copies of the networks of the previous evaluation and train little, with smaller steps: from one evaluation to the next \(\bar m\) changes by thousandths, and the networks hardly change. They are copied so that the earlier ones stay intact.

Tolerance and bias

The networks' \(\Phi\) comes out a little low at every point visited, by 1.3 to \(1.8\cdot 10^{-4}\) docstring: it is a bias rather than noise. That is why the secant stops at \(2\cdot 10^{-4}\), of the order of that error, and not at the grid's \(10^{-10}\).

A fixed bias \(\delta\) moves the fixed point by roughly \(\delta/(1 - \Phi')\), and \(\Phi\) changes little with \(\bar m\) (\(\Phi' \approx -0.065\) docstring).

Calculation by hand

\(-1.5\cdot 10^{-4}/1.065 \approx -1.4\cdot 10^{-4}\). The docstring gives 1.064982 against the grid's 1.065128, a difference of \(-1.5\cdot 10^{-4}\), after 3 evaluations and 60 minutes. It is within the 0.001 we ask for \(\bar m_T\).

Decisions, and why

The shared secant

It only needs values of \(\Phi\), not its derivative. Since \(g\) is almost a straight line, it gets there almost at once. It is the same function the grid uses, so the only difference between the two equilibria is how \(\Phi\) is evaluated.

Warm start from the previous evaluation

About 9 minutes per evaluation instead of 43. It always starts from the last evaluation, which is usually the one with the closest \(\bar m\).

Copies, not the same networks

Each evaluation keeps its networks, which stay intact to be looked at later.

Tolerance 2·10⁻⁴

Asking for more precision than the error of the networks is chasing noise.

Starting from another equilibrium

With previous= it starts from the networks of a nearby equilibrium (for example, another \(\lambda\)), and then all evaluations, the first one included, use the short settings.

At the end it returns \(\bar m\), the three networks at that \(\bar m\) (with their \(\Phi\), the error of the copy and the seconds of each evaluation), the history of pairs \((\bar m, \Phi(\bar m))\) and the total time.

9The check with finite differences

src/mfg/fd/hjb.py · generator.py · fokker_planck.py · equilibrium.py

Have a reference answer to measure the networks against: the same equations, the same edges and the same interface (value, position, density).

How it does it, in five ideas:

  1. A grid of 801 wealths by 401 instants, with the derivatives replaced by differences between neighbours.
  2. The grid as a chain of jumps: each node goes down to the one below at rate \(\alpha_j\) and up to the one above at rate \(\beta_j\) ("luck ∓ drift"). A single piece serves both equations.
  3. The HJB, backwards from \(T\), column by column. In each column it alternates between solving with \(\pi\) fixed and recomputing \(\pi\) with formula (4) (policy iteration).
  4. Fokker–Planck, forwards from \(m_0\), with the transpose of the HJB matrix. The population follows exactly the computed strategy, the mass is 1 up to rounding and the density is never negative.
  5. The same secant, with tolerance \(10^{-10}\).

The networks have been measured against it, according to the docstrings:

  • the V network's \(\pi^*\), within 0.07 for \(t \le 0.9\);
  • the density, within 0.09 for \(t \le 0.75\);
  • \(\Phi(1.1)\): 1.062745 against 1.062810;
  • the equilibrium: 1.064982 against 1.065128.

The details, folded:

The grid, step by step

In each column all nodes are computed at once (implicit step): each node is tied to its two neighbours, so it is a tridiagonal system, almost free. It is stable with 400 steps; a step that only used the known column would need about 7700.

The jump rates of a node \[ \alpha_j = \cR{\frac{(\sigma\pi_j)^2}{2\Delta x^2}} - \cB{\frac{b_j}{2\Delta x}}, \qquad \beta_j = \cR{\frac{(\sigma\pi_j)^2}{2\Delta x^2}} + \cB{\frac{b_j}{2\Delta x}}, \qquad \cB{b_j} = \cB{r x_j + \pi_j(\mu - r)} \]

They have to be positive. With centred differences that requires \((\sigma\pi_j)^2 \ge |b_j|\,\Delta x\) (Péclet condition). If it does not hold, the code switches to "upwind". With \(\Delta x = 0.005\) it is never needed: at the worst node (\(x = 1.1\), \(\pi = 0.214\)) luck is ten times the drift.

The sup of the HJB as a parabola

Inside the sup there is a parabola in \(\pi\). If \(V\) is concave, its maximum is formula (4). With the grid, policy iteration takes exactly that maximum with the grid's differences.
